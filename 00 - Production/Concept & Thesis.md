# Concept & Thesis

## Logline
As AI coding agents eat the craft he lives for, a brilliant, prickly developer named **[[Liam]]** is managed out of his job — priced by a peer feedback form his colleagues fill in honestly. Then the company's agent, still holding a token minted in his name, takes production down while he sleeps. The press decides what he is, officers arrive to collect him, and the trial that was supposed to settle it never finishes. The story is told by **[[Brendan]]**, the one colleague who ever tried to help him.

## The shape
- **Runtime:** ~75 minutes.
- **Cast:** two live speakers — [[Liam]] (Speaker A) and [[Brendan]] (Speaker B, who also narrates). Everyone else — Marcus, Kristina, HR, the CEO, the press, the court, the officers — is an **AI-generated video character**, subject to the live-body question in [[Rework — The Escalation Pass]] §E/§I.
- **Structure:** **one thread, five movements, escalating**. The institutions detach from reality a notch per movement; Liam does not. Running order in [[Structure & Scene Map]]; the contract that keeps the escalation from becoming randomness is the ladder in [[Rework — The Escalation Pass]] §G.
- **The history:** the Luddites are in the play, but nobody visits 1812. **Marcus** — an engineer on the team, politics-obsessed the way Liam is tech-obsessed — drags them into standups nobody asked him to. He is right about the shape and wrong about the details, and Liam corrects him, so the audience gets accurate history through the argument instead of a lecture.
- **Tone:** darkly comic / satirical with real tech-industry teeth. The jokes are funny — and then the same jokes come back when the stakes are real and they aren't funny anymore.

## Thesis (what it's actually about)
This is **not** a play about whether AI is good or bad. It's a play about **what a society does with the people a new machine makes "redundant," and how cheaply it discards craft, dignity, and loyalty**.

Three things the play wants the (hacker / builder) audience to feel by the end:
1. **Recognition.** Liam's standup, his pedantry, his pride in a clever solution — that's *us*. The Luddites weren't anti-technology; they were skilled people destroyed by *how* a technology was deployed. So is Liam. Marcus keeps saying this and nobody listens, including the audience, until it's too late for them too.
2. **Complicity.** The betrayal isn't a cartoon villain. It's ordinary people making locally-reasonable choices that add up to abandonment. The clearest case is Marcus: the man who has correctly diagnosed the whole system is the man who prices Liam out of a job, sincerely, via a survey. The audience should catch themselves on the wrong side.
3. **Unease, not a slogan.** What happens to Liam is *earned and tragic, and also a warning*. We do not hand the audience a hero or a manifesto — which gets harder to hold in the second half, where a court and a set of officers are much easier to boo than a manager. We leave them holding the question.

## The satire, and the curdle
Early: the comedy is **observational** — corporate AI-hype doublespeak, Liam's exhausting correctness, Marcus derailing a standup with the Luddites. Big laughs.

Late: the **identical devices return without the laugh**. The all-hands jingle plays over the layoff. The "we're a family here" line is said by the person signing the severance. Liam's own dismissive line about rogue agents deleting production databases becomes the thing that happens. The anonymised sentence that priced him in calibration is read into a courtroom as evidence. The curdle is the engine of the piece — tracked line-by-line in [[Themes & Motifs]].

## The ending (direction set, last beats open)
Liam is removed to a Salvadoran prison while his trial is still on the calendar. Not a verdict — the courtroom simply becoming irrelevant after the audience has spent twenty minutes learning its rules. The real hook is exact: the 2025 removals were carried out under the **Alien Enemies Act of 1798**, previously invoked three times, the first being the War of 1812 — and the **Frame Breaking Act** is 1812. Marcus says this, accurately, and is treated as a crank.

We do **not** show the prison. We hold on the moment *before* — and let the AI-video chorus, finally, go silent. Cut to black on the question, not the answer. Which specific image carries that is still open; see [[Rework — The Escalation Pass]] §A3.

> Working title: **The Big Hack** (the double meaning is the point — the heist, and the hack-job done to two men). Easy to rename.

## What this play is not
- Not a lecture about AI safety or model capabilities.
- Not pro- or anti-Luddite as a costume; the history is used honestly (see [[Luddites — History]]). Marcus gets the myths wrong on purpose and is corrected on stage, so the play never asserts them.
- Not a tragedy that lets the audience off the hook with a villain to boo — the hardest rule to hold once ICE and a prosecutor are on stage.
- Not a hero's story. A hacker audience will want to cheer the hack, the arrest and the courtroom stunt; decide per beat whether they're allowed the laugh, and where it gets taken back.
