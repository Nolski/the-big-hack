# Concept & Thesis

## Logline
As AI coding agents eat the craft he lives for, a brilliant, prickly developer named **[[Liam]]** is managed out of his job, betrayed by the coworkers he trusted, and radicalised — while two centuries earlier the weaver **[[John]]** loses his trade, his town, and his family to the power loom, and is pulled toward machine-breaking. Two men, two centuries, one machine.

## The shape
- **Runtime:** ~60 minutes (theatrical length, ~20+ scenes).
- **Cast:** two live speakers who *embody* [[Liam]] and [[John]]. Everyone else — coworkers, bosses, wives, magistrates, the machine itself — is an **AI-generated video character**.
- **Structure:** **braided** — we stay in one story for a whole **movement**, build it to a near-climax, cut away at the peak, then build the other, and converge at the end (the Godfather II / Cloud Atlas / Cloud Nine shape; see [[Braided Structure — Reference]] and [[Structure & Scene Map]]). Every Liam beat still has a John twin ([[Beat Sheet — Parallel Intercut|beat sheet]]) — but the audience feels the rhyme *across the cut between movements*, not side-by-side.
- **Tone:** darkly comic / satirical with real tech-industry teeth. The jokes are funny — and then the same jokes come back when the stakes are real and they aren't funny anymore.

## Thesis (what it's actually about)
This is **not** a play about whether AI is good or bad. It's a play about **what a society does with the people a new machine makes "redundant," and how cheaply it discards craft, dignity, and loyalty** — told twice so the audience can't dismiss either version as a one-off.

Three things the play wants the (hacker / builder) audience to feel by the end:
1. **Recognition.** Liam's standup, his pedantry, his pride in a clever solution — that's *us*. The Luddites weren't anti-technology; they were skilled people destroyed by *how* a technology was deployed. So is Liam.
2. **Complicity.** The betrayal isn't a cartoon villain. It's ordinary people — coworkers, neighbours — making locally-reasonable choices that add up to abandonment. The audience should catch themselves on the wrong side.
3. **Unease, not a slogan.** Liam's radicalisation is *earned and tragic, and also a warning*. We do not hand the audience a hero or a manifesto. We leave them holding the question.

## The satire, and the curdle
Early: the comedy is **observational** — corporate AI-hype doublespeak, Liam's exhausting correctness, guild pomp, a snake-oil "automation" booster in a powdered wig. Big laughs.

Late: the **identical devices return without the laugh**. The all-hands jingle plays over the layoff. The "we're a family here" line is said by the person signing the severance. The boosterish loom-demo patter is the magistrate's eulogy for a dead weaver. The curdle is the engine of the piece — tracked line-by-line in [[Themes & Motifs]].

## The ending (direction, not yet scripted)
Both timelines collapse onto the stage at once (the [[Arcadia — Structural Technique|Arcadia move]]): Liam at his keyboard and John at his loom, lit as one silhouette, each about to do the thing that can't be taken back. We do **not** show the catharsis of the act. We hold on the moment *before* — and let the AI-video chorus, finally, go silent. Cut to black on the question, not the answer.

> Working title: **The Big Hack** (the double meaning is the point — the heist, and the hack-job done to two men). Easy to rename.

## What this play is not
- Not a lecture about AI safety or model capabilities.
- Not pro- or anti-Luddite as a costume; the history is used honestly (see [[Luddites — History]]).
- Not a tragedy that lets the audience off the hook with a villain to boo.
