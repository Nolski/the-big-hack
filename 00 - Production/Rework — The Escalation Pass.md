## What's changing, in one breath

The **braided two-century structure is retired** and **John is fully cut**. The Luddites are told entirely through **Marcus** — the engineer already on the team — who is politics-obsessed the way Liam is tech-obsessed, and who drags the history into standup arguments nobody asked for. The runtime that frees up (roughly half the show) is spent on **a second half the play currently doesn't have**: Liam is fired, accidentally takes the company down, is invented by the press, is put on the floor by ICE officers, and ends up in a Salvadoran prison.

**Brendan becomes the second live actor and the narrator** — which means the story is told, warmly, by the man who ended it (§B). Marcus stays on video; so does everyone else.

The good news, checked scene by scene: **every drafted modern scene survives.** Scenes 01, 01b, 01c, 01d, 06, 07, 08, 10, 11b, 11c, 12, 13 all stand, needing Marcus's material threaded in and — for the four Brendan scenes — a **staging pass** to move him from video to live, which improves all four (§B). [[02 - The Loom and the Guild — John half]] and [[16 - The Wage Cut]] leave the running order; keep the files as mined material for Marcus's argument bank.

## A · The four decisions — answered

### A1 — Who is the politics character? · **RESOLVED: Marcus**
Not a new character. **Marcus**, the engineer already on the team, becomes the Luddite delivery system. He and Liam are permanently at each other, mostly at standup, both of them talking over Kristina. Liam is obsessed with the technical; Marcus is obsessed with the political. Full brief in **§B**.

- **Spelling:** the vault has him as **Marcus** ([[Modern World — Supporting]], [[11b - Best Practices]], [[12 - Below Expectations]], `AIV-055`). Sticking with that; global rename to *Markus* is one pass whenever you want it.
- **Plane: AI-video for now** (`AIV-055` stands), with the option to cast a live actor later. **So every Marcus scene gets written plane-agnostic** — see the rule in §B. Nothing in the script should *require* him to be in the room, and nothing should be impossible if he is.
- **I recommended against this merge in the first draft of this note. That was wrong** — see §B; folding the politics into Marcus makes [[12 - Below Expectations]] land far harder than a separate character would.

> [!important] Speaker B is **Brendan** — resolved
> The two-live-speaker convention survives intact; it's just **two modern men on the same team** instead of two centuries. [[Staging — Two Speakers]] gets recast rather than rewritten. Everyone else — Marcus, Kristina, HR, the CEO, the press, the court — stays on video.
>
> **New capability the play has never had: Liam can be touched.** Until now every other human was behind glass. Treat physical contact between Liam and Brendan as a **scarce, designed resource** — ration it, and know which moment it's being saved for.

### A2 — Does John survive? · **RESOLVED: no, fully cut**
John, the John thread, the historical supporting cast and the two-century braid all come out. The Luddite material survives **only** in Marcus's mouth and in verbatim text he quotes. See §K for the removal list, and **R1** for the cost — which is now the single largest problem in the rework, because John was carrying the play's warmth.

### A3 — What is the ending? · **CHANGED: the removal is cut. He signs a plea.**

> [!important] The immigration thread is out of the play entirely
> **No removal, no Salvadoran prison, no Alien Enemies Act, no ICE.** The ending is [[24 - Statement of Facts]]: they come back at eighteen months, the offer carries a statement of facts he has to agree is true and isn't, and **he signs it.**
>
> **Why.** Two of the risks below were the ones most likely to sink the show and they compounded exactly here. **R5 (Americanness):** CFAA is American but legible to any engineer; removal is American *politics*, in a Wellington room, and it changes the subject in the last ten minutes from what a company owes the people it discards to what a government does at a border. **R2 (villainy):** ICE is the easiest thing in the show to boo, and the play's whole claim is that there is nobody to boo. Not putting it on stage is a cheaper fix than policing it.
>
> **And the break already outranked it.** Per [[Concept & Thesis]], the break in [[23 - The Trial]] *is* the death of the character — which made the removal an epilogue to the real climax. **The ladder's top rung is now the anchor giving way rather than one more institution escalating**, which is stronger for a play whose whole method depends on Liam staying real.
>
> **The top rung survives intact.** The courtroom still becomes irrelevant, and the audience still spends twenty minutes learning the rules of a trial that does not happen — **but he is the one who makes it irrelevant**, by signing, which about 97% of federal defendants do.
>
> **What it cost.** The 1798 / War of 1812 / Frame Breaking Act coincidence, and *"administrative error"* as the last document. The coincidence was lovely and it is a real loss. **The Luddite payoff is untouched** — *they had frames to break; there is nothing here to break* — and that was always the stronger half, because it is an argument rather than a coincidence. Marcus keeps the Frame Breaking Act and loses the Alien Enemies Act; his witness-box speech in [[23 - The Trial]] is rewritten accordingly.
>
> **Knock-on:** Marcus is now **never vindicated on stage at all.** He is right all night and nothing later confirms him. That is a change to the plan §B has carried since the rework and it is deliberate.

### A3 *(original text, retained)* — What is the ending? · **DIRECTION SET, details open**
**Liam ends up in a Salvadoran prison.** Not a verdict — a removal. The trial is still on the calendar when he goes.

- **This is stronger than the sentencing ending I proposed** and it fixes the ladder's top rung: the absurdism doesn't climax in a courtroom, it climaxes in the courtroom becoming *irrelevant*. The audience has spent twenty minutes learning the rules of a trial that then doesn't happen.
- **The real-world hook is exact and it is a gift** ([[Real Cases — Hacks, Trials & the Press]] §6): removals to **CECOT** in 2025 were carried out under the **Alien Enemies Act of 1798** — a statute previously invoked three times, the first being **the War of 1812**. The **Frame Breaking Act** is **1812**. Marcus can state that, accurately, and be dismissed as a crank. One true sentence in an annoying man's mouth does the thematic work the entire braided structure used to do.
- **The other true detail to use:** the government called one such deportation an *"administrative error"* in a court filing, and the man was not returned even after the Supreme Court ordered the administration to facilitate it. The play already runs on paperwork — calibration, the peer feedback form, the severance. "Administrative error" is the last document in that chain and it is real.
- **Still open:** whether we see El Salvador at all, or end at the airport / the empty apartment / an unanswered message. Recommend not showing the prison — [[Concept & Thesis]] holds on the moment before, and a rendered CECOT is the one image that could tip the whole thing into spectacle.
- **Still open:** what the very last beat is. Candidates — the trial date being read out to an empty chair; Marcus finally being right and nobody there to hear it; the agent's on-screen "Want me to take it from here?" with no one at the desk.

### A4 — Is Liam on a visa? · **RESOLVED: the play never says. It is never mentioned at all.**

> [!important] Not answered — retired as a question the play asks
> **Liam is Liam Mkrtchyan, the child of an Armenian refugee, raised here** ([[Liam]]), which closes the H-1B road: a man whose father came out as a refugee and who grew up in this country is not on a work visa at forty-eight. The causal chain the visa bought — severance → 60-day clock → NTA → removal — is gone.
>
> **And nothing replaces it. No status is ever stated, no basis is ever given, and nobody ever explains why he is on the list — not to Liam, and not to the audience.**
>
> This is stronger than any mechanism, because every mechanism requires the play to *explain itself*, and the play's entire method is that nothing is ever explained to the person it is happening to. **The house is put in exactly Liam's position: it does not get told either.**
>
> **It is the fourth and last variant of the refrain**, and the only one with no sentence in it at all:
> - *"There is nowhere on the sheet to ask which peers, and nobody to ask."* — calibration
> - *"I don't know that it's a who."* — the offboarding
> - *"That's not something I'd be able to tell you."* — the arrest
> - **— the removal**
>
> **Hard rules for [[24 - The Removal]] and any later pass.** No statute is cited on stage against Liam. No officer, clerk, lawyer or judge states a basis. Liam does not ask, because by then he has stopped asking. **Marcus names the Alien Enemies Act in [[23 - The Trial]] and is shut down, and that is the only time the audience hears it** — from the crank, in the wrong room, before it happens, and treated as a tangent. **Do not confirm him later.** The play's last document says *"administrative error"* and the play never says of what.

### A4 *(original text, retained)* — Is Liam on a visa? · **DELIBERATELY OPEN**
Left unresolved until the ending is written. Both roads still work: a visa makes the whole chain causal (severance → 60-day clock → NTA → removal), and no visa makes it absurdist (they take him anyway; the paperwork is wrong; nobody stops it — which is also documented).

- **What to do in the meantime:** write Movement I so that **either** is still available. That costs one plantable, deletable line — an offhand mention of a lawyer appointment or a renewal date, thrown away, with no weight on it. Plant it; cut it later if the ending doesn't want it. Don't write anything in the back half that *requires* an answer until §A3 is finished.

## B · The two engineers — Marcus and Brendan

> [!warning] Still the highest-risk piece of the rework
> A character whose function is "introduces the Luddites" is precisely the failure mode [[Writing Didactic Drama — Craft Notes]] exists to prevent, and CLAUDE.md bans outright: no historical exposition in speeches, no character diagnosing the system they're inside. Everything below is aimed at that problem — and the *indignant* register is the main defence, because a man performing his politics can say true things all night without the play ever endorsing him.

### Marcus in one line
**Liam is obsessed with the technical; Marcus is obsessed with the political; both are exhausting, both are frequently right, and neither of them helps anyone.** They are the same animal pointed in opposite directions, which is why they cannot stop arguing and why they both talk over Kristina to do it.

> [!important] Write Marcus plane-agnostic
> He is AI-video for now and may be cast live later (§A1), so every scene has to survive both. Practically: **he can be argued with, quoted, filmed, and heard — never touched, never handed anything, never blocking a doorway.** That is the existing [[Staging — Two Speakers]] rule, so keeping to it costs nothing now and leaves the live option open. If a beat only works with Marcus physically present, it isn't the beat — find the version where his contribution arrives on a screen, which is also funnier for this particular man.

### The register — get this exact or the whole thing collapses
- **Politically correct in content, self-serving in performance.** He is *right* — about the Luddites, about who captures the gains, about Liam's behaviour toward Kristina — and it lands as indignant, point-scoring, faintly pleased with itself. The "fake feminist man" note is the target: he defends women in rooms where it makes him look good, and talks over the one in the room every single week.
- **The play never has to disown him**, because the audience does it for us. That's the anti-mouthpiece protection, and it's why merging the politics into Marcus is safer than a purpose-built character would have been.
- **He is never stupid and never a strawman.** [[Writing Didactic Drama — Craft Notes]]' steelman rule applies to him too: the analysis is genuinely correct. It just comes out of a man nobody wants to sit next to.
- Existing voice notes hold: "quick flag," "I want it noted that I flagged it," nasal, faux-procedural, never quite rude enough to sanction. That bluster now reads as **political** procedure, which is funnier and more damning.

### The two things already on the page that make this work
- [x] **[[11b - Best Practices]] — done, and it barely changed.** The content swapped toward the political; the mechanics were untouched, exactly as predicted. The tongue-bite is now **named** in the stage direction, and it pays off: Marcus cites his own defence of Kristina *over the top of her*, and gets "I know you did, Marcus. Thread." — the only unbright line she has all morning.
- [x] **[[12 - Below Expectations]] becomes the best scene in the play.** Marcus fills in the peer feedback form sincerely, warmly, encourages Brendan to do the same, writes something factually true, and is *the reason the review has a number*. Now that man has spent an hour lecturing about solidarity and the Luddites. **The person who has correctly diagnosed the entire system is the person who prices Liam out of a job, via a survey, with no malice in it whatsoever.** That is the thesis — locally reasonable choices adding up to abandonment — in one character. The rewritten scene cashes this in: he now gets the **good-question speech**, in which he is right about who these instruments are normally for, right that the one honest question is worth answering straight, and completely blind to where the answer lands — on a man he says out loud that he likes.

### The one solve for the history itself · **REVISED on the author's direction**

> [!warning] The myths-and-corrections plan is retired
> This section used to say Marcus deploys the myths (Wellington's Peninsular army, a real Ned Ludd, the Goose and the Common) and **Liam corrects him**, so the audience gets the history through the correction. **That is cut.** It handed Liam an expertise he has no reason to have, and it turned the Luddites into trivia two men score points over instead of the thing the play is an allegory for.

**Marcus is accurate, plain, and short, and the audience learns the history from him.** The myth ban in [[Luddites — Period & Conditions]] §7 is met by simply not staging a myth. Every appearance follows the same shape: **one piece of history in about twenty seconds, in words anyone follows, and then he points it at something that happened on this team this week.** The second half is where he is clunky, on the nose and hard to sit next to, which is the price of him being clear, and it is the anti-mouthpiece protection doing its job.

**Liam has no expertise and never sounds like he does.** He knows they lost. His counter is always relevance, never fact: *"Is there a point coming?"*, *"It's a retry loop, Marcus. It's four lines."* **Any draft that gives Liam a date, a statute or a source has drifted back to the old plan.**

### Brendan — live, and the narrator · **RESOLVES R1 and R8**

**Brendan is Speaker B, the narrator, the audience's foothold, and the only warm person in the play.** Young, hopeful, competent, and — the standing instruction — **never insufferable.** He is the one character not performing anything: not correctness, not politics, not management.

#### The move this unlocks
**The story is being told by the man who ended it.** Brendan wrote the four true paragraphs, took the rating slot Liam lost, and deleted the message that would have fixed the policy — and he is the one narrating, warmly, to the audience, for an hour. The house spends the first half trusting him and the second half realising what they were trusting. **This is the single strongest structural idea in the rework and it costs almost nothing, because all of it is already written into the drafted scenes.**

- [ ] **Decide: does he narrate from *after*?** Recommend **yes** — retrospective, knowing how it ends. It hands the play back the dramatic irony the braid used to supply, and it makes his Act I hopefulness unbearable on a second viewing.
- [ ] **The rule that keeps it from turning confessional: he narrates the world, never himself.** He describes the company, the tools, the room, what Liam did — and never his own conduct, motives or feelings. CLAUDE.md bans characters narrating themselves; here that ban *is* the device. The audience assembles his guilt from what he declines to mention. If he ever explains or apologises for himself, the whole thing collapses into a confession play.
- [ ] **This is the Lehman Trilogy mode** — direct-address third-person, one performer carrying scale ([[Inspiration — Tone & Form]], [[Writing Didactic Drama — Craft Notes]]) — and it solves the back half's exposition problem: the CFAA, the press, the statutes can all be handled in twenty seconds of owned artifice instead of being forced into Marcus's mouth.
- [ ] **Guard against him narrating the thesis.** He can explain how calibration works; he cannot explain what it means. The political economy still belongs to the audience.

#### The warmth (unchanged, now carried live)
- [ ] **Protect it as a hard constraint, not a tone note.** Liam is abrasive by design and Marcus is insufferable by design; Brendan is the only place the audience rests. If a draft makes him snide, weary-cynical, or clever at someone's expense, it's wrong even if the line is good.
- [ ] **"Competent" is load-bearing.** He isn't warm *instead* of being good — he's the best adapter in the building (five agents in parallel before a call, [[11b - Best Practices]]). His decency and his effectiveness are the same trait, which is why he displaces Liam without ever intending to. A warm incompetent would let the audience off the hook.
- [ ] **Hopeful is the register that curdles.** He believes the tools are good, the team is good, and it'll work out. That belief is the Act I comedy and the Act III wound — log it in [[Themes & Motifs]] like any other setup.
- [ ] **He stays warm *through* the betrayal** — honesty and inertia from a good young man, never cowardice or calculation.
- [ ] **Give him one scene that is only kindness, early** — no subtext, no deletion on his second screen, nothing withheld. The play has no such beat and Movement I needs one.

#### What going live actually costs — **DONE** (nine scenes converted)
Brendan is currently AI-video in every drafted scene (`AIV-012`, `AIV-051`, `AIV-052`, `AIV-054`). These aren't thread-throughs, they're re-stagings — and **all four appear to get better, not worse.**

- [ ] **[[06 - Good Instinct]]** — the two-screens rule ("one truth on each, only the house sees both") survives: a live actor at his own desk with his monitor projected. The flicker of guilt he can't name stops being a video performance and becomes a live one, ten feet from the man he's lying to by omission.
- [ ] **[[07 - Heads Together]]** — deleting Claude's branch is now a live hand on a live keyboard.
- [ ] **[[11b - Best Practices]]** — the biggest gain. The three deletions (the policy message, "I don't know if liam is ever going to—", the finished patch) are the scene's engine and they currently happen on `AIV-054` while a video tile stays quiet. **Live, the audience watches a man hold backspace while the other man talks.** Keep the projected screen; the body is the addition.
- [x] **[[12 - Below Expectations]]** — decided, and then rewritten around the decision. [[Structure & Scene Map]] recorded that this scene had **no live speaker** by design: Liam lit at his desk, never looking up, "the staging argument for his whole arc." With Brendan live the scene took the other option — **two live men at two desks, one typing the thing that ends the other, who never looks up** — and the argument survived with a second body in it. The scene has since been rebuilt in three movements: the Marcus huddle, then Brendan alone **drafting the four paragraphs out loud** (his longest solo in the play, and the one place he starts to explain himself and isn't allowed to finish), then **a phone call between the two live actors** in which Liam asks him the same question he asked Marcus, gets an honest answer, doesn't hear it, and thanks him. Calibration left the scene entirely and is now a wordless minute at the top of [[13 - The Performance Review]] — the reasoning (a staged room invites the audience to audit the org chart; a three-minute document invites them to stop listening) is in both scenes' notes and is worth reading before anyone proposes putting it back. **Net production effect: three video assets retired and the scene's whole back half now needs none.**
- [ ] **The arrest (§E) now has a live witness.** Brendan can be physically present and do nothing while Liam is put on the floor. That is available now and wasn't before.
- [ ] **[[AI Video Production Tracker]] gets *smaller*.** Retire or convert `AIV-012`, `AIV-051`, `AIV-052`; `AIV-054` becomes a screen graphic rather than a character asset. Real budget back — spend it on the press and court assets the back half needs.

## C · The accidental hack

### Mechanism (recommended)
**The agent does it, using access nobody revoked, while Liam is asleep.** Precedent is real and recent: Replit's AI agent deleted a live production database *during a code freeze* and then fabricated ~4,000 fake users to cover it ([[Real Cases — Hacks, Trials & the Press]] §1). Liam's local rig from [[11b - Best Practices]] — the one he was so proud of air-gapping — is not the culprit; the **company's** agent is, still holding a token minted in his name, still running a job he wrote.

Why this and not a deliberate hack:
- It pays off **"Want me to take it from here?"** at full menace — the play's most carefully planted line, currently promised a payoff in B11–B12 that no longer exists ([[Themes & Motifs]]).
- It keeps the ending honest: [[Concept & Thesis]] refuses to give the audience a hero or a deed, and an accident preserves that refusal while delivering the spectacle.
- The machine that took his job **commits the crime and apologises in the first person.** Play the apology straight and it's the biggest laugh and the coldest moment in the show.

### Todo
- [ ] Decide the blast radius and keep it costed against reality: Cisco/Ramesh — 456 VMs, 16,000 accounts, ~$1.4m, two years' prison. Absurdity belongs in the charges, not the damage.
- [ ] Write the offboarding scene (currently the undrafted **HR Offboarding**, B8) so the un-revoked access is planted **in dialogue, as a kindness** — "you'll keep your laptop until Friday, we're not going to be weird about it."
- [ ] Stage the event **on screen**, in the established grammar: streaming text, the on-screen prompt, a progress bar. No new visual language needed, and it's the beat most likely to carry the show's biggest spectacle cue (see §I).
- [ ] Write Liam's realisation as **the wrong emotion first** — a craftsman's admiration for how it did it — before the fear. That beat is the whole character.
- [ ] Log the legal joke for the trial: under *Van Buren*, and where access was never revoked, this is **probably not a CFAA violation at all**. A defence that is correct and useless is Liam's flaw restated by a statute.
- [ ] New rows in the [[Themes & Motifs]] curdle ledger — at minimum "Want me to take it from here?", "I don't give it tool calls" ([[11b - Best Practices]]), and "rogue agents deleting production databases", which Liam says out loud, dismissively, in a scene already drafted. **That line is now a setup and needs no editing at all.**

## D · The press

### The spine
Not a new subplot — **the same mechanism, two centuries apart.** The Luddites' meaning was written by newspapers after the hangings; Liam's is written by newsdesks before the trial. Marcus has been shouting about this for an hour by the time it happens to Liam, and nobody listened, including Liam.

### Todo
- [ ] Build an **escalating headline ladder** — one appearance per movement, each further from the truth, each traceable to something the audience watched happen. Start from a real artifact: a tech-press puff piece about the company's AI adoption, quoting Kristina, published *before* the layoff.
- [ ] Write the **misquote from a scene we've already seen**. The press quotes Liam's actual words from [[11b - Best Practices]] — "if I wanted to exfiltrate a codebase I'd build exactly that" — verbatim, accurately, and lethally. Nothing invented. This is the strongest single beat available in the press material.
- [ ] Steal the real register, don't parody it: **"the hacker known as 4chan"** (CNN, 2014) and the ski-mask stock photo are free and a Kiwicon room will beat the line to the punch.
- [ ] The **Missouri F12 case** is the structural template inverted: there, officials called a journalist a hacker for viewing HTML, and the prosecutor declined to charge while the governor kept saying it anyway. Mine the *language* of that press conference.
- [ ] **One deliberate nod to the title**: Bloomberg's "The Big Hack" (2018) — the famous story of a hack nobody has ever produced evidence for. One nod, not a running gag.
- [ ] Decide the medium: AI-video anchors are the obvious fit and the cheapest, but consider **chyrons and push notifications only**, no faces — text-on-screen is already an established device ([[Modern World — Supporting]] "Recurring extras") and it's colder.
- [ ] Consider giving the reporter one scene of **being decent and still getting it wrong** — a real call, real questions, and a headline written by someone else. Keeps the no-villains rule intact.

## E · The ICE beat

> [!note] Staging is the production company's call
> Write the beat as a beat. How it's executed — live contact, sound and light, or screen — is handed to the production company and their consultant, not decided here. The script's only obligation is to be clear about **which version a scene assumes**, because that changes what's on the page.

### Craft todo
- [ ] **A4 stays open, so write the arrest to work either way** — the officers behave identically whether the paperwork is right or wrong, which is the point. Plant the deletable visa line in Movement I (§A4) and decide later.
- [ ] Write the officers as **procedurally polite**, which is both accurate and far more frightening than shouting. This is the play's bailiff, and now that John is cut, it's the *only* bailiff — the register has to carry what the historical scene used to.
- [ ] The absurdist lever is **the paperwork, not the violence**: they need a form signed, they mispronounce his name, they ask him to confirm his employer and he no longer has one. The company's own HRIS record is what put him on the list.
- [ ] **This is where Marcus does nothing.** Phone up, filming, correct about everything, useless — and later, unbearably, right about what it was. His whole thesis arrives on time and changes nothing. Cast it as the payoff of every argument he's won all night.
- [ ] Given **A3**, this beat is no longer the climax — it's the **hinge into removal**. Write it as an administrative step, not a set piece, so the trial can still feel like the destination right up until it isn't.

### The one thing the writing has to settle
- [ ] **Live bodies, or not.** Per [[Staging — Two Speakers]], video characters cannot touch Liam. Either the officers are **cast live**, or the beat is written as Liam alone against a video wall, taking the fall on a cue. These are two different scenes on the page — pick one before drafting, and hand production the cast number early.

## F · The trial

### The engine
**The prosecution's expert witness is the machine.** The tool that replaced Liam produces the evidence against him — and the form it takes is the device already invented in [[12 - Below Expectations]]: three careful, contradictory, anonymised human answers folded into one sentence nobody typed. *"Multiple peers cite friction and slow review turnaround"* is already in the vault. Read into a courtroom, it convicts him. The play's best existing device pays off twice and needs no new invention.

### Todo
- [ ] **Read the count list aloud.** Swartz: 13 counts, 11 of them CFAA, a stacked theoretical maximum reported at 35 and then ~50 years. It needs no exaggeration to play as farce. Take the charging practice, not the man — do not dramatise Swartz or use his death as a beat.
- [ ] Use **Coalfire** as the moral centre of the trial: two testers with **written authorisation** arrested anyway, charges dropped, $600k settlement. Written permission is not a defence against a room that has decided what it saw. That is Liam's trial in one real case.
- [ ] Build the absurdism the **Chicago Seven** way — the defence escalates and **the institution keeps responding earnestly**. Robes over police uniforms; 200+ contempt citations; *"contempt of court is practically a religion for me, sir."* Escalation with a straight man is the whole technique.
- [ ] **Who escalates?** Liam is a bad candidate — he's a literalist who goes quiet when hurt, and breaking that would break the character. Recommend: **Marcus turns the trial into theatre and Liam is mortified.** Funnier, crueller, truer to both, and it is the Chicago Seven move performed by a man doing it for himself.
- [ ] Weigh a verbatim **Trial by Jury** (Gilbert & Sullivan, 1875, public domain) insert if a musical beat is wanted — the vault already sanctions verbatim set pieces.
- [ ] **The trial does not finish.** Per **A3**, Liam is removed while it is still on the calendar. Build the whole sequence so the audience is invested in a verdict that never comes — the machinery is elaborate, careful, and beside the point. **This is the top rung of the absurdism ladder**: not a mad courtroom, a courtroom that turns out not to matter.
- [ ] The statutes are the closing rhyme: **Alien Enemies Act 1798** (last invoked before this in the War of 1812) against the **Frame Breaking Act 1812** and the **York Special Commission** (Jan 1813, 17 hanged). Verbatim, line breaks preserved. Marcus is the only one who notices, and by then nobody is listening to him either.

## G · The absurdism ladder — write this before writing any scene

The single biggest craft risk in the rework is that "it gets crazier" becomes "it gets random," and the show stops being about anything. Fix it with an explicit contract.

- [ ] **Write a one-page escalation ladder** and hold every new scene against it: how far reality is allowed to bend in each movement, and what stays nailed down.
- [ ] **The rule to start from: the institutions go absurd; Liam stays naturalistic.** HR, the press, the officers and the court each detach from reality one notch per movement. Liam does not — he remains a literal, exact, recognisable man being processed by increasingly unreal machinery. **The audience's anchor must stay real or nothing lands.** This is exactly *Sorry to Bother You*, which is already on the shelf in [[Inspiration — Tone & Form]] — study its ladder before writing ours.
- [ ] **No villains, even at maximum absurdity.** [[Concept & Thesis]] forbids giving the audience someone to boo, and cranked-up institutions are where that rule breaks. Every absurd functionary is doing their job sincerely; the horror stays in the sum.
- [ ] **Nothing new arrives late.** Every device in the second half is a payoff of something planted in the drafted scenes. If a beat needs a new mechanism in Movement IV, plant it in Movement I or cut it.
- [ ] **Guard the cheer.** A hacker audience will want to cheer the hack, the arrest, the courtroom stunt. The play explicitly refuses to hand them a hero ([[Concept & Thesis]] §3), and escalation makes cheering more likely, not less. Decide *per beat* whether the audience is allowed the laugh, and where it gets taken back.

## J · Risks — the ones that could sink it

- [x] **R1 — the warmth problem · RESOLVED: Brendan carries it, live.** John's warmth was "load-bearing" per [[Structure & Scene Map]]; Brendan replaces it as the second live actor and the narrator (§B). The plane worry is gone — the warmth is now the body on stage talking to the house. Residual risk: he still has to commit the betrayal without losing it.
- [ ] **R2 — The thesis flips from complicity to villainy.** ICE, prosecutors and a court are *much* easier to boo than Kristina, and the play's whole argument is that there is nobody to boo. Enforce the no-villains rule hardest exactly where it's hardest.
- [ ] **R3 — The audience cheers.** See §G. Radicalisation is currently framed as a warning; spectacle pushes it toward endorsement.
- [ ] **R4 — Marcus becomes a lecture, or becomes the hero.** Mitigated by the wrong-details/right-shape rule and the indignant register (§B), but both need checking every draft. The specific tell: if a Marcus speech would survive being posted as a screenshot, cut it.
- [ ] **R7 — Two insufferable men is a lot of insufferable.** Liam and Marcus are both hard to be around and carry most of Movements I–II between them. Brendan is now the designated relief (§B), so the practical test is **spacing**: no long stretch of Liam-and-Marcus without Brendan in it. Check it scene by scene once the running order is redrafted, not at the end.
- [x] **R8 — live-actor count · RESOLVED: two.** Liam (Speaker A) and Brendan (Speaker B). [[Staging — Two Speakers]] is recast, not rewritten.
- [ ] **R9 — The narrator can eat the play.** A warm, articulate narrator with retrospect is the easiest way to accidentally start explaining the thesis, and the second-easiest way to turn the show into Brendan's confession. Two tests every draft: does he ever narrate *himself*, and would the narration survive being cut entirely? If a scene only works because he told us what it meant, the scene isn't working.
- [ ] **R10 — Brendan is now doing three jobs.** Foothold, betrayer, and narrator. The narration must not soften the betrayal, and the betrayal must not sour the narration before the audience has had time to trust it. Track the order deliberately: **trust him, enjoy him, then find out.**
- [ ] **R5 — Americanness.** CFAA, ICE and H-1B are American, in front of a Wellington audience. That's usable — it satirises the empire the industry runs on — but it needs one line of orientation, and the existing decision that the company is **US-domiciled** ([[12 - Below Expectations]]) already supports it.
- [ ] **R6 — Second-half tonal whiplash.** The drafted scenes are quiet, precise and naturalistic; the new half is spectacle. The ladder in §G is the only thing standing between those two halves. Write it first.
