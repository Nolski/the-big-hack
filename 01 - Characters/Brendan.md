# Brendan

> [!abstract] One-line
> A young, capable, genuinely kind engineer — the only person in the play not performing anything — who tells the audience this story and does not mention that he is the reason it ends the way it does.

**Speaker B, and the narrator.** He is the audience's foothold and the play's only warm place. He is also the man who wrote the four true paragraphs that priced Liam out, took the rating slot Liam lost, and deleted the message that would have fixed the policy. The house spends the first half trusting him and the second half working out what they were trusting.

## Snapshot
- **Age / station:** late twenties. A good mid-level engineer, two or three years in, on Kristina's team with [[Liam]] and Marcus. Shipped the developer-portal API by vibecoding it, and it launched to the best feedback of any release.
- **Wants (external):** to do good work, be useful, and have the people around him be alright.
- **Needs (internal):** nothing he could name. He is not in crisis and that is the point — the play's damage is done by someone whose life is going fine.
- **Fatal flaw / blind spot:** **honesty without imagination.** Asked a leading question by a form, he answers it truthfully and does not picture where the answer goes. He believes the tools are good, the team is good, and it'll work out.
- **Voice:** open, modest, conciliatory. Reaches to collaborate even mid-humiliation — "maybe we could go through it together?" Gets cut off. Never snide, never clever at someone's expense.

> [!important] Two hard constraints on every draft
> **Never insufferable.** Liam is abrasive by design and Marcus is unbearable by design; Brendan is the only place the audience rests. If a draft makes him weary-cynical or witty at another character's cost, it's wrong even if the line is good.
>
> **"Competent" is load-bearing.** He isn't warm *instead* of being good — he's the best adapter in the building (five agents in parallel before a call, [[11b - Best Practices]]). His decency and his effectiveness are the same trait, which is why he displaces Liam without ever intending to. A warm incompetent would let the audience off the hook.

## The narrator function

He narrates from **after** — retrospectively, knowing how it ends. That hands the play the dramatic irony the old braided structure used to supply, and it makes his Movement I hopefulness unbearable on a second viewing. The mode is direct-address third person, one performer carrying scale (the *Lehman Trilogy* register — see [[Inspiration — Tone & Form]]), and it solves the second half's exposition problem: the CFAA, the press and the statutes can be handled in twenty seconds of owned artifice.

> [!important] The rule that keeps it from becoming a confession play
> **He narrates the world, never himself.** He describes the company, the tools, the room, what Liam did. He never describes his own conduct, motives or feelings. CLAUDE.md bans characters narrating themselves; here that ban *is* the device — the audience assembles his guilt from what he declines to mention. If he ever explains or apologises for himself, the whole thing collapses.
>
> Two tests every draft: does he narrate *himself*, and would the scene still work with the narration cut? If a scene only works because he told us what it meant, the scene isn't working. He can explain how calibration works; he cannot explain what it means.

> [!warning] The one sanctioned exception — don't let a later pass "fix" it
> There is exactly one place in the play where Brendan turns out and starts to explain himself: **"I want to be clear that I'm not—"**, immediately before he clicks Submit in [[12 - Below Expectations]]. He doesn't finish the sentence and nothing makes him stop; he just stops, and clicks. It is not narration and it is not a confession — it is an apology the play denies him, and it works *because* the rule above is otherwise absolute. **Do not extend the line, do not answer it, and do not add a second one anywhere in the show.** A draft that completes the sentence has thrown away both the beat and the rule.
>
> Everything else in that scene is drafting aloud, not direct address: he is alone at a keyboard reading his own sentences back and revising them, talking to the screen. That is a different mode and it is not restricted.

## Arc

1. **Movement I** — the decent man who gets stepped on. Shipped the thing, offers to walk Liam through it, is talked over. The audience adopts him immediately.
2. **[[06 - Good Instinct]]** — uses the tool carefully, because he actually heard Liam's one legitimate objection. Quietly solves Liam's race condition and lets Liam credit his own instinct. First flicker of guilt he can't name.
3. **[[07 - Heads Together]]** — can't take credit, and being honest to a fault, half-exposes Liam to Kristina anyway. Deletes Claude's branch — live hand, live keyboard.
4. **[[11b - Best Practices]]** — the three deletions: the policy message, "I don't know if liam is ever going to—", the finished patch. Live, this is a man holding backspace while another man talks.
5. **[[12 - Below Expectations]]** — the turn, and his longest solo in the play. Asked a leading question by the peer feedback form, he talks himself through twenty minutes of drafting and, for the first time, doesn't hold backspace. He does it warmly, encouraged by Marcus, with no malice in it. The one deletion he *does* make is the only cruel sentence in the answer, which leaves the rest cleaner and more quotable — he curates toward kindness and builds a better weapon. Then Liam rings him, asks the exact question he asked Marcus, gets an honest answer, doesn't hear it, and thanks him for being the only person who'll tell him anything.
6. **[[13b - The Plan]]** — two minutes of a man trying to warn someone and finding no sentence that survives being said. *"The nine weeks is there now."* Then, again: *"It's there* now." Then nothing, because Liam asks why it wouldn't be there in three months and the honest answer is four paragraphs in March. **Same gesture as the unfinished line before Submit** — a man stopping with nothing stopping him. He never tries a third time.
7. **After** — awkward and avoidant. Messages left on read. He takes the rating slot Liam lost without ever knowing the two things are connected, and in [[14 - The Offboarding]] the promotion lands under the launch applause while Liam is being processed in another room. **Liam congratulates him and means it.** Brendan does not reply, and is not in the scene.
8. **[[19 - Nine Tickets]]** — **he is cut in the same reduction**, six days after the promotion, because his work lived in Linear and the pull was from Jira: tickets closed, nine, in three years. He sent his own laptop back the same afternoon. He tries to tell Liam about the four paragraphs — the third and final attempt — and **Liam stops him out of kindness**: *"You didn't do anything."* Then he asks to borrow a laptop to rebuild his portfolio and Liam gives him the better machine instead. **The handover is the first physical contact in the play** (see [[Rework — Brendan Goes Dark]] §C).
9. **[[20 - The Audit]]** — home, drunk, and furious, with a portfolio folder containing four files and nothing he is allowed to show anybody. He finds Liam's credentials still live eight days after both of them were let go, and he is **right about all of it** — the tokens don't expire, he raised it in February, they gave the agent every key in the building because it was faster. So he asks for a security audit, which is the responsible thing and the thing he had been asking for all year, and then adds **"and prove it."** To prove the list, it opens the doors. He is asleep for eleven thousand of them. **It is the same flaw, escalated**: one true, reasonable instruction and no picture of where it goes, exactly as with the feedback form. Four minutes of work; the machine does the rest. **He must never be written as gleeful, competent or vengeful at that keyboard.**
   - **Why he breaks and Liam doesn't.** Liam's worth is internal and a spreadsheet cannot reach it. Brendan's has only ever been external — the review, the promotion, the applause — and with the company gone there is nothing underneath: *"He knows he's good. I've got a review that says I'm good. And the review's from them."* That is what "and prove it" is.
10. **The arrest** — he can be physically present and do nothing. Liam is charged for it.

> [!important] He is the only person in this play who finds out what he did
> The standing rule is that nobody does — Marcus never learns he wrote the number, Kristina never learns what her deletion cost, and Brendan never learns that the four paragraphs and the rating slot were the same event. **The hack is the single exception, and it has to be**, because he is the narrator. He has been telling this story warmly for an hour *knowing*, and the rule that he **narrates the world and never himself** is what keeps it from becoming a confession. That rule is now carrying the heaviest load in the play. Do not soften it and do not let him get near it.

He stays warm **through** the betrayal. It is honesty and inertia from a good young man, never cowardice or calculation. Track the audience's journey deliberately: **trust him, enjoy him, then find out.**

## Staging
Live, Speaker B. In every drafted scene he and Liam are remote colleagues on a call — separate desks, separate pools of light, no shared props. The two live actors *can* touch, which no two characters in this play ever could before; it is being saved. See [[Staging — Two Speakers]].

His two screen assets stay as **screen graphics**, not character clips: `AIV-044` (the Claude session) and `AIV-054` (the plain session, the three deletions). They matter more now that a live body is sitting at them.
