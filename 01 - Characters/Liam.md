# Liam

> [!abstract] One-line
> A brilliant, exacting software developer who loves the *craft* of solving hard problems — and cannot survive in a world that has decided the craft no longer needs him.

**Mirror twin:** [[John]] — both are *masters of a craft* made redundant by a machine, betrayed by their own community, and radicalised. Liam is the prickly genius; John is the simple family man. Same fall, different men, so the audience can't write it off as one guy's bad attitude.

## Snapshot
- **Age / station:** ~38. Staff(-ish) engineer at a mid-size, well-funded modern software company. No direct reports — by choice. The person other engineers quietly route the *hard* bugs to.
- **Wants (external):** to keep doing the work he's great at, on his terms, and be respected for it.
- **Needs (internal):** to be *seen* — to have his craft and care recognised as worth something by people he (secretly) wants to belong with. He'd never say this out loud.
- **Fatal flaw / blind spot:** he believes **being right is the same as being safe**. He thinks excellence is a shield. He is socially oblivious to the fact that the room decides his fate, not the code. He mistakes politics for noise.
- **Voice:** precise, qualifying, parenthetical. Corrects people mid-sentence. Long pauses, then a burst. Uses exact words ("that's not a bug, that's *undefined behaviour*"). Funny without meaning to be. Goes quiet and literal when hurt.

## Biography
Self-taught before he was credentialed; the kind of kid who read the language spec for fun. Genuinely gifted — not a savant fantasy, a *person* who has put 20 years into being very good at one hard thing. He has shipped systems that quietly hold the company up. He measures his life in **elegant solutions**: the joy is the puzzle, the click of a problem yielding. He is not anti-AI in principle — he's curious about it — but he resents being told the thing he loves is now a "workflow."

He has fewer friends than he thinks. He has been at the company long enough to believe the people around him are *his people*. They are not, quite. That gap is the whole tragedy.

## What the craft is *for* him
Liam was never naturally good with people. He didn't grow up with much community, and not much support — connection was the thing that never came easily. What did come was the machine: computers, analytical work, the clean logic of a hard problem yielding. He was *good* at it, and being good at it is where he found meaning. It's the thing he's proud of — maybe the only thing he's ever let himself be proud of.

And it became his way *to* people, too. He couldn't flatter, couldn't work a room, couldn't do the easy social warmth other people seemed born with — but he could **help**. He could fix the thing nobody else could fix, and through that he made friends. Or thought he did. The craft was both his pride and his bridge: how he contributed, how he mattered, how he let himself believe he was doing something that might be worth something to the world.

That is what makes the agent's arrival a *double* death. AI doesn't just take the job — it takes the one language he had for belonging. And the cruelty is built in: the very deficit that made the craft so precious to him — the missing empathy, the social blindness — is also what makes it nearly impossible for him to *say* so. He can't turn the loss into something a room will feel. He grieves in the one dialect no one around him speaks, so it comes out as friction, pedantry, superiority — never as the grief it actually is.

> [!warning] Where to put him — audience positioning
> Liam sits **between** two poles: a figure the audience openly despises (the smiling axe / the betrayer) and a figure they can purely pity (a clean, blameless victim). He is *neither*. He's hard to love — prickly, superior, his own worst advocate — and yet what's being done to him is real and unjust. Keep him in that uncomfortable middle: the audience should never be fully allowed to root for him *or* to write him off. That unresolved tension is the point — it's what stops the play from being a simple "poor sympathetic worker" story (that's [[John]]'s register; Liam is the harder sell, on purpose).

## Arc
Start: **king of the workshop** — the person who solves what no one else can (B1–B4). The agent arrives and is, infuriatingly, *good* — fast, tireless, "good enough" (B5). The work reorganises around it; Liam's value is reframed from "solves hard problems" to "reviews the machine's output" (B5–B6). He fights it the only way he knows — by being *more correct, more thoroughly* — which reads as friction (B7). The coworkers he counted on go quiet rather than defend him; one actively throws him under (B6, B8). He's managed out under a smile (B8). Isolation (B9). Online, he finds people who name his betrayal and aim his anger (B10). He chooses to act (B11–B12). We end on the brink — not the deed.

- **[[Modern World — Supporting|Sam]]** — coworker Liam considered a friend; goes quiet at the decisive moment. The betrayal is mostly *silence*, not villainy.

*Not a relationship — a force:* the AI coding tool deployed in Liam's workplace is not a person and not a character — it has no lines and never speaks to anyone; the humans use it and talk about it. (Staging notes in [[Modern World — Supporting]].)
