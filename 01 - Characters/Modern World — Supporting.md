# Modern World — Supporting Cast (AI Video)

Liam's world — which is now the only world. All of these are **AI-generated video characters** (see [[AI Video Production Tracker]]), with one exception: **Brendan is live** and has his own note, [[Brendan]]. Functions and pairings are in [[Character Mirror Map]].

## Kristina — the PM / manager *(velocity over craft; the smiling axe)*
- **Function:** the **same upbeat face across the whole fall** — she runs the standup early and owns Liam's review and exit later. In the opener she celebrates the just-launched developer portal (best feedback of any release) and thanks Brendan for grinding the weekend to ship it. **Not clueless** — when Liam attacks the API-key choice she owns it with real conversion data (keys convert better than OAuth), then steps in to shield Brendan; her usual deflection (smoothing friction into a *scheduling problem*) **fails for the first time** here, and Liam's credential jab leaves her speechless. Later she genuinely *likes* Liam, "advocates" for him in calibration, and **signs the severance anyway** — not a villain, a decent person executing a rational machine. This is where the betrayal is most *systemic*: the person who celebrated the launch is the person who manages him out, and means well at every step. Her instinct to reward raw velocity is the soil the layoff grows in.
- **Voice:** brisk, upbeat delivery-speak ("velocity," "quick wins," "activation," "let's take that offline") and modern-management euphemism ("I want to be transparent with you," "let's double-click on that," "this isn't a reflection of your value"). The kindness is real and useless.
- **Function in the pairing:** the smiling axe — see [[Character Mirror Map]].
- **Beats:** B2 (opener — celebrates the portal, thanks the weekend grind, owns the API-key call, left speechless; and the debrief where she first clocks the machine's reach and *warns herself not to* — [[07 - Heads Together]]); B5 (the win becomes the mandate — [[10 - The Win We Needed]]; the standup she can't control and the defense of Liam she wins and then privately abandons — [[11b - Best Practices]], [[11c - The Latest Model]]); B6 (calibration — she advocates, loses, and trades him for Brendan — [[12 - Below Expectations]]); B7 (the review — [[13 - The Performance Review]]); B8 (offboarding).

## THE CEO — the pressure from above *(not greed — fear)*
- **Function:** carries the company's survival. Genuinely relieved by the launch, genuinely hears the engineers' concerns, and genuinely can't afford them — turns "one good weekend" into the **agent-first mandate**, not from malice but from a bad dashboard and a hard boardroom. The decent man at the top of the machine; the pressure that rolls down through Kristina to Liam. Role-based (unnamed, like HR), but written warm and human so the steelman lands.
- **Voice:** warm, big-picture, founder-optimistic, fear underneath. "This is the one we needed." "I can't walk back in there and say some of the engineers have reservations."
- **Function in the pairing:** the pressure from above — see [[Character Mirror Map]]. The board is the thing he can't argue with, the way the market was the thing no mill owner would admit to having built.
- **Beats:** B5 — the win becomes the mandate ([[10 - The Win We Needed]], `AIV-091`); then, weeks on in the same scene's coda, the mandate becomes a *method* — he authorises the headcount cut and accepts Kara's "consistent" Jira metric because it means *"it isn't personal, it's just consistent"* ([[10 - The Win We Needed]], `AIV-094`). Same man, same fear, one quarter colder.

## Marcus — the second lead *(and the play's whole relationship to history)*

> [!important] He is not a supporting face any more
> Marcus carries the Luddites. When John was cut ([[Rework — The Escalation Pass]] §A1), the history didn't go with him — it moved into the mouth of the engineer already on the team. Everything this entry used to say (deliberately ordinary, a face in the grid, a Tuesday) is wrong. He is the [[Character Mirror Map|mirror]] to [[Liam]] and second only to him in stage time across Movements I–II.

- **In one line:** Liam is obsessed with the technical; Marcus is obsessed with the political; both are exhausting, both are frequently right, and neither of them helps anyone. They are the same animal pointed in opposite directions, which is why they cannot stop arguing and why they both talk over Kristina to do it.
- **The register — get this exact or the whole thing collapses.** **He is not performing, and he never scores a point on purpose.** He genuinely cannot read a room and does not know it, and he believes — sincerely, without irony, and in the face of all evidence — that if a true thing is said out loud and written down, something happens. He is *right* about the Luddites, about who captures the gains, about Liam's behaviour toward Kristina, and it still lands as badly-timed, on-the-nose and faintly unbearable, because it arrives at the wrong moment, at length, over the top of somebody. He defends the woman in the room and talks over her while he does it, and he would be genuinely hurt to be told so. The play never has to disown him because the audience does it for us; that is the anti-mouthpiece protection, and it is why folding the politics into an existing socially awkward man is safer than building a new character for it.
- **The specific tell to check every draft:** if a line reads as him *enjoying a win*, it is the old smug register and it is wrong. He is earnest, slightly desperate, and always about four seconds behind the room.
- **Never stupid, never a strawman.** The steelman rule in [[Writing Didactic Drama — Craft Notes]] applies to him too. The analysis is genuinely correct. It just comes out of a man nobody wants to sit next to.
- **He genuinely admires Liam, and the friction is social rather than competitive.** He thinks Liam is the best engineer he has ever worked with and says so, sincerely, twice ([[14a - Checking In]], [[23 - The Trial]]) — and it should be visible in Movement I too, in a small technical deference Liam does not notice. **They are not rivals.** He simply cannot be in a meeting with Liam without ending up across the table from him, because he is odd, he over-explains, and he mistakes every disagreement for an invitation to keep going. That is what makes the fight in [[11b - Best Practices]] sad rather than tiresome: two men who like each other, arguing in dialects that don't convert.
- **The conflicted adopter — the dimension the early drafts missed entirely.** He is the **heaviest and fastest user of the tool on the team** (three tickets merged in a morning, [[06 - Good Instinct]]) and it frightens him. He is not anti-machine and never has been; he is a man who is better at his job than he has ever been and likes the job less, and cannot say why. **This leaks out one line per scene and is never a thesis** — nobody follows it up, he never resolves it, and he never connects it to anything he argues in public. Liam's *"Marcus pastes our source into a stranger's computer and hits accept"* is true, and Marcus knows it before Liam says it.
> [!important] Revised on the author's direction — he is the teacher, and he is accurate
> An earlier plan had Marcus confidently deploying the myths (Wellington's Peninsular army, a real Ned Ludd, the Goose and the Common) so that **Liam could correct him**, with the audience getting the history through the correction. **That is retired.** It gave Liam an expertise he has no reason to have, and it turned the Luddite material into trivia the audience watches two men score points over instead of the thing the play is actually about.

- **He is the audience's teacher, and everything he says is true.** The myth ban in [[Luddites — Period & Conditions]] §7 is satisfied by simple accuracy: he never states a myth, so nobody has to correct one. **The audience should leave the theatre understanding the Luddites better than they did, and they should have learned it from him.**
- **Plain words, short, and always landed on the room.** No dates, no statutes, no troop numbers, no reading list. He explains one thing at a time in about twenty seconds, in language anyone follows, and then points it at something that happened on this team this week. The pattern is fixed: **history, then the thing in the room.** That second half is where he is clunky and on the nose and slightly unbearable, and that is the price of him being clear.
- **The play is the allegory; he is the one holding it up.** He is not decorating the story with a historical parallel. The story *is* the parallel, and he is the only character who can see it. Every argument he loses is one the audience is quietly winning on his behalf.
- **Liam is not an expert and never sounds like one.** He knows they lost. That is all he knows and all he needs, because his weapon is not the history, it is relevance: *"Is there a point coming?"*, *"It's a retry loop, Marcus. It's four lines."*, *"I'm trying to read something."* **If a draft gives Liam a date, a statute or a source, cut it.**
- **Voice:** nasal, constant volume, never quite rude enough to sanction. "Quick flag." "I want it noted that I flagged it." **Read that as a man with no other model of how to be heard, not as bluster** — he wants a record because a record is the only mechanism he trusts, and there has never been one. Which is what makes [[12 - Below Expectations]] so bad: the one time the channel works, it ends Liam.
- **Plane: AI-video for now** (`AIV-055`), with a live casting option held open — so **write him plane-agnostic**. He can be argued with, quoted, filmed and heard; never touched, never handed anything, never blocking a doorway. If a beat only works with him physically present, it isn't the beat — find the version where his contribution arrives on a screen, which is funnier for this particular man anyway.
> [!important] Arc — revised on the author's direction. The old "he is a fixed point" spec is retired.
> Earlier drafts had him **identical in scene 2 and scene 20** while the world moved under him. **That is cut.** He now has a real arc: he gets steadily more radical across the play, and **the engine is survivor guilt.**

- **He does not get more radical because he thinks harder. He gets more radical because the firings happen and he is left behind** — eleven rows above the line, because he files everything in Jira ([[14 - The Offboarding]]) — and he has no way to hold that.
- **The governor is that he is too self-absorbed to know what to do with it.** He cannot say *"I'm sorry it was you and not me."* So he converts the feeling into the only material he owns — history, systems, the shape of the injustice — and processes it **out loud, at the man who is the reason for it.** That is the whole character in one mechanism, and it is why he is unbearable without ever once being insincere.

| Stage | Where | What the guilt is doing |
|---|---|---|
| **0 — none** | [[01 - Cold Open — The Standup]], [[06 - Good Instinct]], [[11b - Best Practices]], [[12 - Below Expectations]] | Ordinary workplace grievance and an uneasy adopter. He has nothing to feel guilty about yet, and the Luddite material is correspondingly small. |
| **1 — the survival** | [[14 - The Offboarding]] | Liam and Brendan go; Marcus is above the line. **He does not appear. The absence is the beat.** |
| **2 — it arrives and is immediately converted** | [[14a - Checking In]] | The flash of guilt, then a lecture. **The lecture *is* the guilt-processing** — he can't say the personal thing, so he says *"there used to be an understanding."* *"I've got a thing at five"* is fleeing the feeling, not callousness, and *"send me your CV"* is the payment he never makes. |
| **3 — it has become a worldview** | [[22 - Current Employer]] | *"obviously I've still got a job, I'm aware of how that sounds, I'm not…"* is the closest he ever comes to naming it, and he swerves into theory mid-sentence. *"THEY'RE GOING TO HANG US ALL"* is a man writing himself into a punishment he escaped. |
| **4 — it asks somebody else to pay it** | [[23b - Continuity]] | *"If it was me I'd do it. I'm not being funny about it."* He wants Liam to go to prison so that his own survival means something. **He does not know that is what he is asking.** |

> [!important] The irony to protect above everything: the guilt is attached to the wrong crime
> **He feels bad for surviving. He should feel bad for the sentence he wrote** in [[12 - Below Expectations]]. He will never know about that, so the feeling fastens onto the only fact he can see, and radicalises him about *that* instead. **He never learns he wrote the sentence that ended Liam, and he should not** — that rule is now load-bearing twice over, because it is the reason the guilt lands on the wrong thing. Every scene after [[14 - The Offboarding]] is a man doing penance for something he didn't do, while the thing he did do sits in a survey nobody can trace.

- **Guards on the arc.** **He never names it** — no line where he says he feels guilty, and nobody says it to him; it shows only in what he does instead (talking more, offering things he won't deliver, needing Liam to be angry). **He never asks Liam a single question about what Liam actually needs** — check every scene from [[14a - Checking In]] on. **It must not make him rescuable:** the guilt explains him, it does not excuse him, and it can never read as an apology the audience accepts on his behalf. And **nothing in the play ever confirms him** — he gets more right, more radical and more compromised all at once, and no character or institution concedes any of it.
- **Guard against him becoming the hero.** A Kiwicon audience will be tempted to adopt the one man who was right. Every time he is right, he should also be doing something small and unbearable. The specific tell to check every draft: **if a Marcus speech would survive being posted as a screenshot, cut it.**

### Where his arguments go (escalating)
- **Movement I — [[01 - Cold Open — The Standup]]. The word only, and no history at all.** First appearance, pure comedy, zero stakes. He mistakes Liam's anti-AI rant for solidarity and is refused in one line — and the refusal is where the word arrives, *used loosely at him*. He objects to the usage, gets one sentence into saying what it actually means, and Liam guillotines it by finding API tokens in a diff. **He never explains, and nobody asks.** The audience leaves Movement I wanting the answer, which is the whole point of the ramp; the first real history is [[06 - Good Instinct]].
- **Movement I — ✅ written, [[01 - Cold Open — The Standup]].** He calls out Liam's credential jab at Kristina. He is right, indignant, sincere, and helps her not at all: he fills the silence *she* was going to fill, she has to spend her recovery thanking and shutting up the man defending her, and **Liam is never addressed at all.** Liam's "I'd absolutely have asked you that" is true and misses the whole point. The whole register in about eight lines.
- **Movement I/II — [[06 - Good Instinct]]. The first history in the play, and it arrives grievance-first.** He does not open on the Luddites and must never sound like he did homework — he opens on **his own week**, on the quality of what this team is now shipping, and the history arrives at the end as *evidence for a complaint*, not as a premise. All of it accurate: they broke some machines and left others standing, they went after the ones turning out cheap goods fast in untrained hands, the cloth got worse and the men who could tell were the ones let go. Twenty seconds, plain words, and then he lands it on **Brendan's shipped PR** without saying Brendan's name, on a call Brendan is on. Liam, headphones half on and not really in the meeting, offers the one piece of history he has: ***"They lost, by the way."*** Planted here, thrown away, paid back at the end.
- **Movement II — ✅ written, [[11b - Best Practices]].** Who captures the gain, staged as a fight about one billing-webhook ticket and never about capitalism: three shipped tickets bought him three more, and the saved week got filled back up by Tuesday. **He concedes first** — yes, he is the heaviest user in the building, yes, every day, and he is the fastest he has ever been — *and then makes the point anyway*, which is the most human thing he does in Movement II. **Liam refuses the frame outright** (*"I'm not a politician, I'm an engineer, and I have a threat model"*) **and Marcus audibly changes into Liam's language to get heard**, then loses in borrowed words. The concession is what makes the switch land: he cannot win in his own dialect partly because his own dialect indicts him. Then, after one line of peace about Grok, he asks for thirty seconds and gets out **Enoch Taylor**, who made the frames and made the hammers, and *"we're the blacksmith."* **This is the beat the whole thread is built toward.** A tile marks a ticket Done while he says it, and Liam answers with the line count.
- **Movement III — [[14a - Checking In]]. The pivot, and stage two of the guilt.** *"A flash of guilt shoots over Marcus"* is already in the scene and it should now drive everything after it: **the lecture is the guilt-processing**, not teaching. Marcus is the only person in the play who says out loud that something was *done* to Liam, and Liam rejects the framing, because accepting it would mean he was never safe. The diagnosis arrives and the patient refuses it. Two pieces of plain history, both landed on the room: an understanding that stopped without being repealed, and a machine that was illegal until the law was removed instead. He dials in from the office, tells a man who has just lost his job what he ought to be feeling, is asked ***"At who?"*** and has no answer for the only time in the play. **He does not know he wrote the number, and the scene never goes near it.**
- **Movement III/IV** — after the firing, he is the radicaliser, and the surprise of the rewrite is **who he actually radicalises.** Liam refuses him flat in [[14a - Checking In]]. **Brendan, sitting silently on the same call, does not.** The seed is three lines long, nothing is decided, and nobody plans anything: they were forbidden to meet about wages, so they met at night about machines; *"did it do anything?"*; *"it made the owners very frightened for about a year."* Two scenes later Brendan is drunk and holding a machine ([[19 - Nine Tickets]] → [[20 - The Audit]]). **He gets to be exhilarated by a situation he helped cause and he never finds out he caused it**, which is now true of him twice over.
- **Movement V** — the material stops being a bit. In the witness box he makes the point that the **Frame Breaking Act** took Parliament eleven months to make machine-breaking a hanging offence, *"and they have never once moved that fast for a person"* — true, on-thesis, and shut down mid-sentence by a judge being perfectly reasonable ([[23 - The Trial]]). **The Alien Enemies Act material is cut with the removal** ([[Rework — The Escalation Pass]] §A3).
- **Movement V — ✅ written, [[23b - Continuity]]. The material is spent here and gets nothing back.** The evening after the trial he calls, lit up, and **reads Liam's eighteen months as the same injustice done to the croppers**: a law written fast to protect a machine, seventeen hanged at York in a single January, and never once the other way in two hundred years. **He is right, and it is the largest thing anyone says in the play.** Then he begs Liam to be the one who picks it back up, tells him *"if it was me I'd do it"* from a job he still has, and promises a gallery for a hearing that will never happen. **Liam refuses him without ever saying he's wrong** — "okay," "no," "I'm going to go" — and signs on Thursday. **Nothing confirms Marcus, then or ever.**
- **He is never vindicated.** With the removal gone, nothing in the play ever confirms him. He is right all night, about everything, and the show ends without a single institution or character conceding it. **That is deliberate — do not add a late beat that proves him right.**

### The two scenes already on the page that make this work
- **[[11b - Best Practices]] — ✅ done.** The Marcus/Liam standup fight sustains itself, resolves nothing, and files both men under "friction." The argument's *content* has been swapped toward the political and the mechanics were untouched, exactly as predicted. He still talks over Kristina twice while she literally bites her tongue — she takes the minutes every week and Liam has just announced nobody does — and it is now **named** in the stage direction, and then paid off: he cites his own defence of her ("I'm the one who said something when he did the degree thing to you") *over the top of her*, and gets the only unbright line she has all morning: **"I know you did, Marcus. Thread."**
- **[[12 - Below Expectations]] is where the merge pays off.** He fills in the peer feedback form sincerely, encourages Brendan to do the same, writes something factually true, and is the reason the review has a number — after an hour of lecturing about solidarity. The scene now gives him the **good-question speech**: nobody has asked him what actually slows him down in three years, the surveys only ever ask how he *feels* about it, and this year somebody finally put a real question on the form. He is right about all of it. He is right about who these instruments are usually for, and right that the answer is worth giving straight, and the one time the channel works it destroys the person he was defending in the same breath ("I like Liam, I'm not being funny about it"). **He never learns he wrote the sentence that ended Liam, and he should not.** Register: dial the `AIV-055` aggression out — enthusiastic, not annoyed, which is a warmer setting and not a quieter one. **This is the scene where the new register is easiest to hear:** he is being a good colleague, he likes his job, and he is doing the one civic thing the company has ever asked of him. His "I want it noted that I flagged it" finally finds a place where things get noted, and it's a survey; he says so out loud and it's the last thing he says before he logs off.

### Consider
- **He films the arrest**, and that footage is what the press cuts to mean the opposite. The sympathetic witness supplies the libel, which is exactly what this man would do instead of intervening. Works on either plane — the clip simply exists afterwards with his voice on it.
- **The press device and Marcus are the same argument:** the word "Luddite" was manufactured by newspapers after Mellor's hanging ([[Real Cases — Hacks, Trials & the Press]]). He has been complaining about that for an hour by the time it happens to Liam.
- **He turns the trial into theatre and Liam is mortified** — the Chicago Seven move performed by a man doing it for himself. Liam is a bad candidate for escalation; he's a literalist who goes quiet when hurt.

## HR / "People Partner" — the system
- **Function:** runs the offboarding. **She has a name — Dana — and she uses it, and Liam uses it back at the end.** The human face of an inhuman procedure. **First and only appearance in person is the exit** ([[14 - The Offboarding]]) — but she is *present* twice before, both times as text: the pasted protective paragraph in the calibration thread (the prologue to [[13 - The Performance Review]]) — a plan, thirty/sixty/ninety, written criteria and dates before anyone sits Liam down — and then the fork email in [[13b - The Plan]]. No face, no voice. **Read these as risk-management, not warmth:** a documented plan and a released, generous severance are what protect the *company*, and she sends the same boilerplate to everyone. The offer in [[13b - The Plan]] is still genuinely generous (that must not become a trick), but it is generous *policy*, not Dana advocating for Liam.
- **She is not warm and not cruel — she is the script with a face on it, and that is the horror.** In the offboarding she reads the legally-required part and, every time Liam opens something real, **closes it gently and returns to process.** She tells him the plan and the reduction are separate; withholds the criteria ("that's not something I'm able to share"); deflects who chose ("it isn't really something that sits with a person"); and routes his biggest question to the pack and the outplacement provider. Her job is to get through this with the least possible exposure to the company, and she does it competently and without malice. **She never advocates for him, never volunteers a straight answer about the decision, and never gets a beat of smugness or relief.** The euphemism is not her being fake — it is the only vocabulary the job supplies. Per [[Performance Management — How It Actually Works]]: HR is the euphemism engine that makes an arbitrary decision sound responsible; she is not the cruelty. Hold that register.
- **The register in one beat: the routed question.** He asks what he could have done differently — and instead of an answer he gets pointed at a PDF and a provider. Then he supplies the real diagnosis himself, badly, on his own — more rooms, friendlier — and she says she's *"really not able to speak to that."* **She isn't scoring a point and isn't being bleak; engaging the merits is the one thing the conversation is built to avoid.** The cruelty is entirely structural: the nicest thing available to her is a routing, and the routing is the answer. **The thesis that there is no field for what he does is no longer voiced by anyone** — it lives in the prologue sheet (no column for reviews) and in Liam's own line in [[19 - Nine Tickets]]. Let the spreadsheet carry it.
- **She never raises her voice, never rushes him, and never fills his silences.** Much of her work in the offboarding is waiting. When he loses the thread she does not finish his sentences for him; when he gets an arithmetic answer wrong she says his name once and stops. **Not-mean and not-warm are both active choices** — she is neither unkind nor his friend.
- **Voice:** scripted, even, immovable. Institutional deflection phrases ("I hear you," "I know that's frustrating," "that's not something I'm able to go into," "it's all in the pack"). Unhurried — she has done this many times.
- **Function in the pairing:** the system with a face — see [[Character Mirror Map]]. The officers in Movement IV are the same register escalated one notch, and they are now the play's only bailiff.
- **Beats:** the calibration thread (text only — the wordless prologue to [[13 - The Performance Review]]); the fork email (text only — [[13b - The Plan]]); then in person, **twice**, in [[14 - The Offboarding]] (`AIV-074`) and [[14b - Three Weeks]] (`AIV-096`).

> [!important] She runs the same call twice, and the repetition is the argument
> **The script does not change and neither does she.** Brendan gets the identical read-out, the identical deflections and the identical next-steps sequence five days later, delivered at the same speed with the same emphasis — and he *argues*, loudly, with a genuinely excellent case, which Liam never did. **It changes nothing, and she is neither harder on him for shouting nor softer on him for being right.** `AIV-074` and `AIV-096` must be performed identically; if the second read-out sounds different from the first, both scenes lose their point.
>
> She gets exactly two new moves in the second scene and both are inside the register. She says **"it didn't"** when he hears himself say something embarrassing — two words of social repair, not comfort, and not a comment on his worth. And she says **"Brendan."**, once, to stop him saying something about her he can't take back: **the only time she interrupts anybody in the play.** Flat, no volume. Both readings of it — kindness, and closing the subject — are correct and the actor should not choose.

> [!warning] Register changed — the warm/advocating Dana is retired
> Earlier drafts had Dana as *"the most useful person in the play,"* warm and honest, telling Liam *"I don't think there was anything"* and *"I don't think it would have changed the number,"* and offering a personal reference. **That version is cut.** On the author's direction she is now cold in the specific sense of **task-focused on a liability-minimising conversation** — never mean, but never his advocate, and giving him nothing about the decision. If a draft anywhere gives her warmth about his worth, honesty about the criteria, or a personal offer, it has drifted back to the old register. The play keeps its no-individual-villain thesis: her coldness is the institution speaking through a person, not personal malice.

## Kara — Head of Product *(the sane skeptic who supplies the instrument)*
- **Function:** Kristina's boss, reports to the CEO. Introduced in [[08 - The One-on-One]] (cut in the cut pass; she now enters at the reduction coda) as the last skeptic with power — dry, seasoned, distrustful of the CEO's enthusiasms. **Her load-bearing beat is now the reduction-method coda in [[10 - The Win We Needed]]** (`AIV-095`): handed a headcount cut, she lays out the two ways to run it — manager-led vs. a single consistent metric — names the real problems with the manager-led path (slow, inconsistent, "it came down to who had the best advocate"), and proposes pulling activity out of **Jira** and sorting by it. **She is giving sane advice to a real problem and she is not the villain.** The metric genuinely removes manager bias; it is also the blunt instrument that cannot see reviews or Linear, and it is the origin of the `AIV-076` reduction sheet.
- **The irony she cannot see:** the road she rejects — involving the managers — is the only road on which Kristina's calibration advocacy could ever have mattered. By choosing the consistent metric, the company builds the process that ignores its own managers, which is why the review never decides anything. See [[Performance Management — How It Actually Works]] and [[Rework — Brendan Goes Dark]] §B.
- **Voice:** dry, unhurried, corporate-pragmatic; deadpan about leadership's whims, dead serious about what gets promised and how it's defended.
- **Beats:** the 1:1 ([[08 - The One-on-One]], `AIV-047`); the reduction-method coda ([[10 - The Win We Needed]], `AIV-095`, reusing the `AIV-047` vector).

> [!note] The AI coding tool is not a character
> The tool that displaces Liam is a **force** the humans use and talk about, not a figure to cast or voice — its menace is in how it's *deployed*, not in any personality. It appears only as on-screen UI / screen captures (see [[AI Video Production Tracker]] and [[Staging — Two Speakers]]); anything that looks like it "talking" is written as stage direction, never dialogue. Keep its look consistent — a warm corporate-synthetic interface, streaming text, a default on-screen prompt of "Want me to take it from here?" — so the late-play menace is earned.
