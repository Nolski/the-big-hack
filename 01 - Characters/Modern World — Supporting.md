# Modern World — Supporting Cast (AI Video)

Liam's world — which is now the only world. All of these are **AI-generated video characters** (see [[AI Video Production Tracker]]), with one exception: **Brendan is live** and has his own note, [[Brendan]]. Functions and pairings are in [[Character Mirror Map]].

## Kristina — the PM / manager *(velocity over craft; the smiling axe)*
- **Function:** the **same upbeat face across the whole fall** — she runs the standup early and owns Liam's review and exit later. In the opener she celebrates the just-launched developer portal (best feedback of any release) and thanks Brendan for grinding the weekend to ship it. **Not clueless** — when Liam attacks the API-key choice she owns it with real conversion data (keys convert better than OAuth), then steps in to shield Brendan; her usual deflection (smoothing friction into a *scheduling problem*) **fails for the first time** here, and Liam's credential jab leaves her speechless. Later she genuinely *likes* Liam, "advocates" for him in calibration, and **signs the severance anyway** — not a villain, a decent person executing a rational machine. This is where the betrayal is most *systemic*: the person who celebrated the launch is the person who manages him out, and means well at every step. Her instinct to reward raw velocity is the soil the layoff grows in.
- **Voice:** brisk, upbeat delivery-speak ("velocity," "quick wins," "activation," "let's take that offline") and modern-management euphemism ("I want to be transparent with you," "let's double-click on that," "this isn't a reflection of your value"). The kindness is real and useless.
- **Function in the pairing:** the smiling axe — see [[Character Mirror Map]].
- **Beats:** B2 (opener — celebrates the portal, thanks the weekend grind, owns the API-key call, left speechless; and the debrief where she first clocks the machine's reach and *warns herself not to* — [[07 - Heads Together]]); B5 (the win becomes the mandate — [[10 - The Win We Needed]]; the standup she can't control and the defense of Liam she wins and then privately abandons — [[11b - Best Practices]], [[11c - The Latest Model]]); B6 (calibration — she advocates, loses, and trades him for Brendan — [[12 - Below Expectations]]); B7 (the review — [[13 - The Performance Review]]); B8 (offboarding).

## THE CEO — the pressure from above *(not greed — fear)*
- **Function:** carries the company's survival. Genuinely relieved by the launch, genuinely hears the engineers' concerns, and genuinely can't afford them — turns "one good weekend" into the **agent-first mandate**, not from malice but from a bad dashboard and a hard boardroom. The decent man at the top of the machine; the pressure that rolls down through Kristina to Liam. Role-based (unnamed, like HR), but written warm and human so the steelman lands.
- **Voice:** warm, big-picture, founder-optimistic, fear underneath. "This is the one we needed." "I can't walk back in there and say some of the engineers have reservations."
- **Function in the pairing:** the pressure from above — see [[Character Mirror Map]]. The board is the thing he can't argue with, the way the market was the thing no mill owner would admit to having built.
- **Beats:** B5 — the win becomes the mandate ([[10 - The Win We Needed]], `AIV-091`); then, weeks on in the same scene's coda, the mandate becomes a *method* — he authorises the headcount cut and accepts Kara's "consistent" Jira metric because it means *"it isn't personal, it's just consistent"* ([[10 - The Win We Needed]], `AIV-094`). Same man, same fear, one quarter colder.

## Marcus — the second lead *(and the play's whole relationship to history)*

> [!important] He is not a supporting face any more
> Marcus carries the Luddites. When John was cut ([[Rework — The Escalation Pass]] §A1), the history didn't go with him — it moved into the mouth of the engineer already on the team. Everything this entry used to say (deliberately ordinary, a face in the grid, a Tuesday) is wrong. He is the [[Character Mirror Map|mirror]] to [[Liam]] and second only to him in stage time across Movements I–II.

- **In one line:** Liam is obsessed with the technical; Marcus is obsessed with the political; both are exhausting, both are frequently right, and neither of them helps anyone. They are the same animal pointed in opposite directions, which is why they cannot stop arguing and why they both talk over Kristina to do it.
- **The register — get this exact or the whole thing collapses.** Politically correct in content, self-serving in performance. He is *right* — about the Luddites, about who captures the gains, about Liam's behaviour toward Kristina — and it lands as indignant, point-scoring, faintly pleased with itself. He defends women in rooms where it makes him look good and talks over the one in the room every single week. The play never has to disown him because the audience does it for us; that is the anti-mouthpiece protection and it is why folding the politics into an existing annoying man is safer than building a new character for it.
- **Never stupid, never a strawman.** The steelman rule in [[Writing Didactic Drama — Craft Notes]] applies to him too. The analysis is genuinely correct. It just comes out of a man nobody wants to sit next to.
- **Wrong about the details, right about the shape.** He confidently deploys the myths — more troops sent against the Luddites than Wellington had in the Peninsula, a real man called Ned Ludd, the Goose and the Common as an old rhyme — and Liam, pedantic and also correct, corrects him. The audience gets accurate history **through the correction**, nobody lectures, and [[Luddites — History]]'s myth ban is satisfied exactly as written: the myths are staged as things a man believes, not things the play asserts. It is also the funniest version, and it lets Liam win arguments he is going to lose in every way that matters.
- **Voice:** nasal, faux-procedural, constant volume, never quite rude enough to sanction. "Quick flag." "I want it noted that I flagged it." That bluster now reads as *political* procedure, which is funnier and more damning.
- **Plane: AI-video for now** (`AIV-055`), with a live casting option held open — so **write him plane-agnostic**. He can be argued with, quoted, filmed and heard; never touched, never handed anything, never blocking a doorway. If a beat only works with him physically present, it isn't the beat — find the version where his contribution arrives on a screen, which is funnier for this particular man anyway.
- **Arc: none. He is a fixed point** — identical in scene 2 and scene 20 while the world moves under him, until the unchanged position becomes unbearable. He never learns he wrote the sentence that ended Liam, and he should not.
- **Guard against him becoming the hero.** A Kiwicon audience will be tempted to adopt the one man who was right. Every time he is right, he should also be doing something small and unbearable. The specific tell to check every draft: **if a Marcus speech would survive being posted as a screenshot, cut it.**

### Where his arguments go (escalating)
- **Movement I** — first appearance, pure comedy, zero stakes: derails a standup with a tangent nobody wants. Plants "Luddite" as an insult he objects to being used loosely.
- **Movement I** — he calls out Liam's credential jab at Kristina, which is already written into [[01 - Cold Open — The Standup]]. He is right, indignant, and helps her not at all — she was handling it, and now it's about him. The whole register in about six lines.
- **Movement I/II** — the argument Liam wins. Liam corrects a myth, scores, and dismisses the frame entirely: *"they lost, by the way."* It comes back at the end.
- **Movement II** — after the agent-first mandate: who captures the gain, staged as a fight about one specific ticket, never about capitalism. Kill any line that could be a tweet.
- **Movement II** — after [[12 - Below Expectations]]: Marcus is the only person who says out loud that something has been *done* to Liam, and Liam rejects the framing, because accepting it would mean he was never safe. The diagnosis arrives and the patient refuses it. **Marcus does not know he wrote the number.**
- **Movement III/IV** — after the firing, he is the radicaliser: a colleague aiming Liam's anger, live, instead of an anonymous forum. He gets to be exhilarated by a situation he helped cause.
- **Movement V** — the material stops being a bit. In the witness box he makes the point that the **Frame Breaking Act** took Parliament eleven months to make machine-breaking a hanging offence, *"and they have never once moved that fast for a person"* — true, on-thesis, and shut down mid-sentence by a judge being perfectly reasonable ([[23 - The Trial]]). **The Alien Enemies Act material is cut with the removal** ([[Rework — The Escalation Pass]] §A3).
- **He is never vindicated.** With the removal gone, nothing in the play ever confirms him. He is right all night, about everything, and the show ends without a single institution or character conceding it. **That is deliberate — do not add a late beat that proves him right.**

### The two scenes already on the page that make this work
- **[[11b - Best Practices]] barely changes.** The Marcus/Liam standup fight is written, sustains itself, resolves nothing, and files both men under "friction." Swap the argument's *content* toward the political and the mechanics are untouched. Also already staged: he talks over Kristina twice while she literally bites her tongue — she takes the minutes every week and Liam has just announced nobody does. The man who would call out sexism is the one doing it. All it needs is naming.
- **[[12 - Below Expectations]] is where the merge pays off.** He fills in the peer feedback form sincerely, encourages Brendan to do the same, writes something factually true, and is the reason the review has a number — after an hour of lecturing about solidarity. The scene now gives him the **good-question speech**: nobody has asked him what actually slows him down in three years, the surveys only ever ask how he *feels* about it, and this year somebody finally put a real question on the form. He is right about all of it. He is right about who these instruments are usually for, and right that the answer is worth giving straight, and the one time the channel works it destroys the person he was defending in the same breath ("I like Liam, I'm not being funny about it"). **He never learns he wrote the sentence that ended Liam, and he should not.** Register: dial the `AIV-055` aggression out and leave the point-scoring in — enthusiastic, not annoyed, which is a warmer setting and not a quieter one. His "I want it noted that I flagged it" finally finds a place where things get noted, and it's a survey; he says so out loud and it's the last thing he says before he logs off.

> [!note] Spelling
> The vault has him as **Marcus** throughout ([[11b - Best Practices]], [[12 - Below Expectations]], `AIV-055`). A global rename to *Markus* is one pass if wanted — do it before the argument bank gets built out, not after.

### Consider
- **He films the arrest**, and that footage is what the press cuts to mean the opposite. The sympathetic witness supplies the libel, which is exactly what this man would do instead of intervening. Works on either plane — the clip simply exists afterwards with his voice on it.
- **The press device and Marcus are the same argument:** the word "Luddite" was manufactured by newspapers after Mellor's hanging ([[Real Cases — Hacks, Trials & the Press]]). He has been complaining about that for an hour by the time it happens to Liam.
- **He turns the trial into theatre and Liam is mortified** — the Chicago Seven move performed by a man doing it for himself. Liam is a bad candidate for escalation; he's a literalist who goes quiet when hurt.

## HR / "People Partner" — the system
- **Function:** runs the offboarding. **She has a name — Dana — and she uses it, and Liam uses it back at the end.** The human face of an inhuman procedure. **First and only appearance in person is the exit** ([[14 - The Offboarding]]) — but she is *present* twice before, both times as text: the pasted protective paragraph in the calibration thread (the prologue to [[13 - The Performance Review]]) — a plan, thirty/sixty/ninety, written criteria and dates before anyone sits Liam down — and then the fork email in [[13b - The Plan]]. No face, no voice. **Read these as risk-management, not warmth:** a documented plan and a released, generous severance are what protect the *company*, and she sends the same boilerplate to everyone. The offer in [[13b - The Plan]] is still genuinely generous (that must not become a trick), but it is generous *policy*, not Dana advocating for Liam.
- **She is not warm and not cruel — she is the script with a face on it, and that is the horror.** In the offboarding she reads the legally-required part and, every time Liam opens something real, **closes it gently and returns to process.** She tells him the plan and the reduction are separate; withholds the criteria ("that's not something I'm able to share"); deflects who chose ("it isn't really something that sits with a person"); and routes his biggest question to the pack and the outplacement provider. Her job is to get through this with the least possible exposure to the company, and she does it competently and without malice. **She never advocates for him, never volunteers a straight answer about the decision, and never gets a beat of smugness or relief.** The euphemism is not her being fake — it is the only vocabulary the job supplies. Per [[Performance Management — How It Actually Works]]: HR is the euphemism engine that makes an arbitrary decision sound responsible; she is not the cruelty. Hold that register.
- **The register in one beat: the routed question.** He asks what he could have done differently — and instead of an answer he gets pointed at a PDF and a provider. Then he supplies the real diagnosis himself, badly, on his own — more rooms, friendlier — and she says she's *"really not able to speak to that."* **She isn't scoring a point and isn't being bleak; engaging the merits is the one thing the conversation is built to avoid.** The cruelty is entirely structural: the nicest thing available to her is a routing, and the routing is the answer. **The thesis that there is no field for what he does is no longer voiced by anyone** — it lives in the prologue sheet (no column for reviews) and in Liam's own line in [[19 - Nine Tickets]]. Let the spreadsheet carry it.
- **She never raises her voice, never rushes him, and never fills his silences.** Much of her work in the offboarding is waiting. When he loses the thread she does not finish his sentences for him; when he gets an arithmetic answer wrong she says his name once and stops. **Not-mean and not-warm are both active choices** — she is neither unkind nor his friend.
- **Voice:** scripted, even, immovable. Institutional deflection phrases ("I hear you," "I know that's frustrating," "that's not something I'm able to go into," "it's all in the pack"). Unhurried — she has done this many times.
- **Function in the pairing:** the system with a face — see [[Character Mirror Map]]. The officers in Movement IV are the same register escalated one notch, and they are now the play's only bailiff.
- **Beats:** the calibration thread (text only — the wordless prologue to [[13 - The Performance Review]]); the fork email (text only — [[13b - The Plan]]); then in person, **twice**, in [[14 - The Offboarding]] (`AIV-074`) and [[14b - Three Weeks]] (`AIV-096`).

> [!important] She runs the same call twice, and the repetition is the argument
> **The script does not change and neither does she.** Brendan gets the identical read-out, the identical deflections and the identical next-steps sequence five days later, delivered at the same speed with the same emphasis — and he *argues*, loudly, with a genuinely excellent case, which Liam never did. **It changes nothing, and she is neither harder on him for shouting nor softer on him for being right.** `AIV-074` and `AIV-096` must be performed identically; if the second read-out sounds different from the first, both scenes lose their point.
>
> She gets exactly two new moves in the second scene and both are inside the register. She says **"it didn't"** when he hears himself say something embarrassing — two words of social repair, not comfort, and not a comment on his worth. And she says **"Brendan."**, once, to stop him saying something about her he can't take back: **the only time she interrupts anybody in the play.** Flat, no volume. Both readings of it — kindness, and closing the subject — are correct and the actor should not choose.

> [!warning] Register changed — the warm/advocating Dana is retired
> Earlier drafts had Dana as *"the most useful person in the play,"* warm and honest, telling Liam *"I don't think there was anything"* and *"I don't think it would have changed the number,"* and offering a personal reference. **That version is cut.** On the author's direction she is now cold in the specific sense of **task-focused on a liability-minimising conversation** — never mean, but never his advocate, and giving him nothing about the decision. If a draft anywhere gives her warmth about his worth, honesty about the criteria, or a personal offer, it has drifted back to the old register. The play keeps its no-individual-villain thesis: her coldness is the institution speaking through a person, not personal malice.

## Kara — Head of Product *(the sane skeptic who supplies the instrument)*
- **Function:** Kristina's boss, reports to the CEO. Introduced in [[08 - The One-on-One]] as the last skeptic with power — dry, seasoned, distrustful of the CEO's enthusiasms. **Her load-bearing beat is now the reduction-method coda in [[10 - The Win We Needed]]** (`AIV-095`): handed a headcount cut, she lays out the two ways to run it — manager-led vs. a single consistent metric — names the real problems with the manager-led path (slow, inconsistent, "it came down to who had the best advocate"), and proposes pulling activity out of **Jira** and sorting by it. **She is giving sane advice to a real problem and she is not the villain.** The metric genuinely removes manager bias; it is also the blunt instrument that cannot see reviews or Linear, and it is the origin of the `AIV-076` reduction sheet.
- **The irony she cannot see:** the road she rejects — involving the managers — is the only road on which Kristina's calibration advocacy could ever have mattered. By choosing the consistent metric, the company builds the process that ignores its own managers, which is why the review never decides anything. See [[Performance Management — How It Actually Works]] and [[Rework — Brendan Goes Dark]] §B.
- **Voice:** dry, unhurried, corporate-pragmatic; deadpan about leadership's whims, dead serious about what gets promised and how it's defended.
- **Beats:** the 1:1 ([[08 - The One-on-One]], `AIV-047`); the reduction-method coda ([[10 - The Win We Needed]], `AIV-095`, reusing the `AIV-047` vector).

> [!note] The AI coding tool is not a character
> The tool that displaces Liam is a **force** the humans use and talk about, not a figure to cast or voice — its menace is in how it's *deployed*, not in any personality. It appears only as on-screen UI / screen captures (see [[AI Video Production Tracker]] and [[Staging — Two Speakers]]); anything that looks like it "talking" is written as stage direction, never dialogue. Keep its look consistent — a warm corporate-synthetic interface, streaming text, a default on-screen prompt of "Want me to take it from here?" — so the late-play menace is earned.
