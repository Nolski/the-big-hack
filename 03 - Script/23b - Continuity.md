# Scene 23b — Continuity

> [!info] Beat
> The evening after [[23 - The Trial]]. Marcus calls, exhilarated, to tell Liam he has a case worth making. **Liam has already decided to sign.** Marcus reaches for the punishment of the Luddites, meaning it as encouragement, and hands Liam the exact reason not to bother. **The last conversation Liam has with anybody before [[24 - Statement of Facts]].** Ladder position: [[The Escalation Ladder]], Movement V.

## Purpose
**To spend the Luddites, finally and completely, and get nothing back for them.**

Marcus has been building to this for two hours of stage time. Here the material stops being a bit: the trade was destroyed, the law was rewritten to make resisting it a hanging offence, seventeen men were hanged in a single January and others were shipped to the other side of the world, and it did not work. **He tells that story as inspiration.** He is trying to hand a frightened man a lineage. And what Liam takes out of it, correctly, is that these people lost everything and the machines stayed, which is what he said at a standup a year ago when it cost him nothing to say.

**The undercut is the whole scene, and it is one question.** Liam asks him their names. Marcus produces one — Mellor — and *"I'd have to look"* for the other sixteen. **This is the play keeping its promise that he is never vindicated** ([[Modern World — Supporting]]) — he is right all night, about everything, and he cannot hold the thing he is right about steady enough to say who it happened to. **Neither man makes anything of it.** Liam is not scoring; he is genuinely asking, because he is about to become a number himself and he wants to know whether the numbers keep their names. **No pause for effect, no reaction, and the scene moves straight on.**

**Nothing is argued and nothing turns.** Liam has already broken, one scene earlier, and this is what a broken man sounds like being encouraged.

## Setting / Staging
Evening, the same day as the trial. **Liam's flat, emptier than [[19 - Nine Tickets]] left it.** The desk is bare. The Mac Studio is gone, because he gave it away. The laptop is gone, because it is an exhibit. **The courier box is gone too, and nobody says where it went** — the returns he refused to send were collected in the end, by people with a warrant, which is not the same thing and is the only joke in the scene.

He is in the suit from court, minus the jacket. He has not eaten.

**Marcus** on video (`AIV-099`), somewhere with people behind him. He has had a drink. It is the best day he has had in a year and he does not know that is what it is.

Cold, low light. No music anywhere in this scene.

## Live Cast

- **LIAM** — live, Speaker A, and **alone on stage for the only sustained stretch in the play.** Courteous, slow, entirely spent. He is not sad and he is not bitter; he has simply stopped expecting anything he says to alter anything, which is the break in [[23 - The Trial]] operating on him in real time. **He concedes nothing, adopts nothing, and argues with nothing.** He lets Marcus talk, answers what he is asked, and tells him the truth once. Do not play grief and do not play defeat as heaviness. Play tiredness and good manners.

> [!note] Live/video plane
> One live man and one face on a screen, and this is the last time Liam speaks to another human being in the play. **[[24 - Statement of Facts]] has counsel, and counsel is a screen too.** No contact of any kind.

## Notes
- **This is the last human conversation in the play and it is a man being recruited.** Marcus is not consoling him. He is asking him for something, and what he is asking for is that Liam go to prison on purpose so that a court has to say a true thing out loud. **He is completely sincere and he is not wrong about the injustice.** He is also safe, employed, slightly drunk, and doing it down a phone line at somebody who signs on Thursday.
- **Stage four of the guilt arc, and the top of it.** Everything since [[14 - The Offboarding]] has been a man who survived and cannot hold it. Here the guilt finally asks somebody else to pay it: **he needs Liam to fight so that his own survival means something**, and he does not know that is what he is asking. *"If it was me I'd do it"* is not bravado, it is the arithmetic of a man who was not chosen, said out loud to the man who was. **Nobody in the play ever names this, including him.** See [[Modern World — Supporting]] for the full ladder.
- **Changes 2026-09-09.** The treason line is cut: it was the only mention of treason in the play, nothing planted it, and the 151 carries the deal on its own ("Eighteen months, Marcus. Against a hundred and fifty-one."). Marcus gets one observation about the company, a fine, a share price, and a model encrypted in evidence, which is the real one's ending ([[Warning Shot — Cotra on the Hugging Face Swarm]]: the model was shuttered and encrypted). It is an observation, not a vindication, and it follows the scene's rule that he is right and it changes nothing. **The names exchange in the notes above is not in the script**; the cut list still names it and should be read as design, not draft.
- **The undercut is one line, and it must be thrown away.** *"If it was me I'd do it. I'm not being funny about it. If it was me I would do it."* **Nobody says anything about it, then or ever.** He would not. He files everything and he'll be fine, which two other scenes have already told the audience. No pause on it, no reaction from Liam, and the play never returns to it.
- **He is never vindicated, and this is where the promise is kept.** [[Modern World — Supporting]] and [[Rework — The Escalation Pass]] §A3 both require that nothing in the play ever confirms Marcus. He has been right about the machinery for two hours. Here he is right about the largest thing in the show — that the law has always moved fast for the machine and slow for the person, and that a number has been put on a man for something a computer did while he slept — **and it does not move Liam an inch, and the play ends four minutes later with a signature.** Do not add a later beat that says he was heard.
- **He said "eleven months" in the witness box that morning** and was told to make it to the question ([[23 - The Trial]]). **He does not repeat it here** — an earlier version of this note claimed he did, and the scene is better without it: by evening the statute has been replaced by the hangings, which is a bigger and more desperate card. **Play him as a man who has one good argument and no way to place it, reaching for a louder version of it.**
- **Liam refuses without disagreeing, which is the whole point.** He never says Marcus is wrong, because he doesn't think Marcus is wrong. He says "okay," and "no," and "I'm going to go." **He does not thank him, does not ask him anything, does not touch the history and does not score off him.** Any line that lets him be sharp here belongs to an earlier Liam and has to go.
- **The worst thing in the scene is Liam's own arithmetic.** Fourteen months with good conduct, low security, a library, courses, people get longer for driving into somebody, he'll be fifty. **He has looked all of it up and he has said it to himself many times before tonight.** The research reflex that made him excellent, aimed at making his own sentence survivable. **Play it calm and slightly rehearsed, never plaintive**, and give the actor nothing to cry with. The audience does that part.
- **"It's not that bad."** The last thing he ever says about his own life in the play, and it is a man talking himself down off nothing. It rhymes directly with *"right, no, that makes sense"* in [[14 - The Offboarding]], where he also accepted the least sensible thing that ever happened to him without writing it down.
- **The phone at the end.** He finds a page he has clearly found before and reads it completely, the way he reads everything. **The play does not say what it is and nobody may indicate it.** It is the prison. He is checking again.
- **Sequencing and handoff.** This sits between [[23 - The Trial]] and [[24 - Statement of Facts]], and the light change at the end goes straight into that scene's bare room, so **Brendan's "ninety-seven percent" narration lands on an audience who have just watched Liam decide.** It also means Marcus knows about the plea before counsel formally puts it, which is right: he is always slightly ahead and it never helps.
- **Brendan is not in this scene and the absence is deliberate.** [[Structure & Scene Map]] warns against long stretches of Liam and Marcus without him, and this breaks the rule knowingly, once, at the end, because **the play is now taking people away from Liam one at a time.** Marcus is the last one, and the reason Brendan cannot be here is that he is the reason any of it happened.
- **Curdle Ledger (logged in [[Themes & Motifs]]):** "they lost, by the way" ([[06 - Good Instinct]]) pays off here without a word from Liam, in Marcus's account of the hangings. "I want it noted / on the record" (Marcus, four scenes) pays off in his own mouth: he cannot name the men he is asking another man to be brave like. And **"I will sit in that gallery every single day"** joins "send me your CV, I'll put it about" ([[14a - Checking In]]) in the ledger of warm sentences nobody ever actions — **there is no gallery, because on Thursday he signs and the hearing comes off the calendar.**
> [!warning] Accuracy — checked against [[Luddites — Period & Conditions]] §5 and §7
> **Verified and safe to stage:** the Frame Breaking Act made frame-breaking a capital felony; seventeen men were hanged at York in January 1813, fourteen of them on a single morning (16 January). **George Mellor** was among those hanged at York in January 1813 and is the one name Marcus can produce — see [[Real Cases — Hacks, Trials & the Press]] §2, where the newspapers coin "Luddite" as a label after his hanging. **Re-verify the Mellor date before staging**; the point of the beat is that he can name one man and not the other sixteen, and it survives with any correctly-sourced name.
>
> **⚠️ Arithmetic to re-check before staging:** the dates in this block (first attack 11 March 1811 → royal assent 20 March 1812) are **twelve months and nine days apart, not eleven.** The "eleven months" figure is asserted in [[22 - Current Employer]] and now in [[23 - The Trial]] as well, so **if it changes it changes in three places.** Either re-source the eleven-month window (the bill's own passage through Parliament in early 1812 was far quicker than either figure) or move all three to "a year." **Flagged, not silently changed.**
>
> **Marcus's one interpretive claim** is that the law has always moved faster for machines than for people. That is his reading, said in his voice, and the play does not assert it. **It is also the line he was shut down on in [[23 - The Trial]]**, so the audience has already heard a judge decline to engage with it.
>
> **Nothing here relies on a myth.** No Ned Ludd, no Wellington comparison, no dating of the Goose and the Common. **An earlier draft had a confident "fourteen years, Australia" for transportation and a Byron exchange; both are cut** — the first because sentences varied and the vault doesn't have it sourced, the second for length.

- **If it has to come down,** cut the four-messages boast at the top and shorten the hangings speech to the two sentences about York. **Do not cut "It's not that bad," the names exchange, "If it was me I'd do it," "Be the one who doesn't," or "I'm going to go."**
