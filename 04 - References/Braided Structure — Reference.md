# Braided Structure — Reference

> [!quote] Why it matters for *The Big Hack*
> We're moving from beat-for-beat *side-by-side* cross-cutting to a **braided narrative**: stay in one story long enough to build it toward a near-climax, **cut away at the tension peak**, build the other to its own peak, switch back — longer movements, not beats — and let the two threads finally **converge at a shared climax**. This is the canon of works that do that well.

## The technique, named
- **Braided narrative.** Two (or more) plot threads with different protagonists, rotated through in **scenes/sections/movements** (not beats), usually converging at a shared climax. The threads "rhyme" — each illuminates the other. ([Patricia C. Wrede on braided stories](https://pcwrede.com/pcw-wp/braided-stories/) · [Rachel Neumeier](https://www.rachelneumeier.com/2025/12/02/braided-narratives/))
- **Cut on the cliffhanger.** Leave each thread at a moment of maximum tension/unresolved question so the audience *carries* it into the other thread. ([September C. Fawkes on cliffhanger types](https://www.septembercfawkes.com/2021/03/the-backbone-of-cliffhangers-4-types.html))
- **The sync problem (watch for this).** Braided threads should sit at *equivalent tension positions* when you cut between them, even when their calendar-time is centuries apart. Cut Liam at the same emotional altitude John is about to reach. (Wrede)

## The exemplars (study these)

**The Godfather Part II (1974) — THE gold standard for our exact shape.** Two timelines — Vito's *rise* (1901–) and Michael's *descent* (1958–) — intercut in **long movements**, with **no flashback signposting**: both play as present-tense stories. Coppola cuts so the eras share a thematic spine (Vito vs. Fanucci ↔ Michael vs. Senator Geary). Contrasting arcs (ascent vs. descent) make the cuts *mean* something — exactly our Liam-falls / John-falls rhyme. → [Screenplay analysis](https://screenplayhowto.com/screenplay-analysis/the-godfather-part-ii-1974/) · [SparkNotes editing](https://www.sparknotes.com/film/godfather/section2/)

**Cloud Atlas — David Mitchell (2004 novel; 2012 film).** Six nested stories, each **interrupted at its climax** before the next begins, then completed in reverse after a central hinge. The purest demonstration of "stop at the peak and make them wait." (Mitchell credits Calvino's *If on a winter's night a traveller*.) Our version is simpler — two threads, fewer switches — but the *interrupt-at-the-peak* principle is the same. → [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_Atlas_(novel)) · [Booker Prizes](https://thebookerprizes.com/the-booker-library/features/everything-you-need-to-know-about-cloud-atlas-by-david-mitchell)

**Cloud Nine — Caryl Churchill (1979) — the THEATRE precedent.** A two-act **contrapuntal** structure: Act I in Victorian colonial Africa, Act II in 1979 London — one long block per era, the second answering the first across time. Proof this works *on stage*, in big movements, with actors doubling across eras. (Churchill again — cf. *Serious Money* in [[Inspiration — Tone & Form]].) → [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_9_(play)) · [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/cloud-9/summary/)

**Christopher Nolan — cross-cutting to a converging climax (Dunkirk, Inception, The Dark Knight).** The cinematic master of **mounting separate streams of tension and ramping them into one**. *Dunkirk* runs three timelines of different lengths (a week / a day / an hour) that **converge**; *Inception*'s last act is one giant cross-cut of parallel levels climaxing together. The model for our **final movement**, where Liam's and John's threads collapse into a single shared climax. → [Cinematic style of Christopher Nolan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinematic_style_of_Christopher_Nolan) · [Dunkirk structure](https://narrativefirst.com/articles/dunkirk-and-the-separation-of-structure-from-storytelling)

**Serialized POV cliffhangers — Dickens; G.R.R. Martin's *A Song of Ice and Fire*.** Rotating point-of-view chapters, each **ending on a hook** that pulls you through the next character's thread. The original engine of "build, cut, switch, build." Useful for pacing the *internal* rhythm of each movement so every switch lands on a question.

**Arcadia — Tom Stoppard (already in the vault).** Still our model for the **convergence**: the simultaneous both-eras-on-stage finish. See [[Arcadia — Structural Technique]]. (Arcadia cuts at the *beat* level; we keep its ending move but adopt the *movement*-level braid from the works above.)

## How we apply it (the rules for *The Big Hack*)
1. **Movements, not beats.** Each switch comes after a multi-scene movement, not after one beat. See the movement map in [[Structure & Scene Map]].
2. **Cut at the peak.** End every movement at a near-climax / unanswered question (Liam about to be cut / John about to break), then switch.
3. **Rhyme the altitude.** When we cut from Liam to John, John should be heading into an *equivalent* emotional peak — the Godfather II thematic-spine trick.
4. **Converge at the end.** Keep the threads separate until the final movement, then collapse them together (Nolan + [[Arcadia — Structural Technique|Arcadia]]).
5. **Keep connective tissue.** The keyboard-clack ↔ loom-clack transition motif ([[Staging — Two Speakers]]) carries each hand-off so the braid feels woven, not stapled.
