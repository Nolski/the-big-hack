# Luddites — Period & Conditions

> [!quote] Why it matters for *The Big Hack*
> A big part of [[John]]'s story is to **educate the audience on the world the Luddites lived in** — so that his fall reads not as one unlucky man's bad luck but as the local edge of a whole system: enclosure, empire, slavery, and a new industrial capitalism backed by the state. This is the comprehensive briefing. The quick-reference version is [[Luddites — History]]; quotable period texts are in [[Primary Sources — Luddite Era]].

> [!warning] Accuracy first — this teaches a live audience
> Several popular "facts" about this period are **contested or wrong**. They're flagged inline as **⚑** and collected in [§7 Accuracy flags](#7-accuracy-flags). Honour them — the play's credibility depends on getting the history right and staging the *debates* as debates.

The five strands below are one system. Read them as a circuit: **slave-grown cotton → mechanised British mills protected by state and Navy → captive colonial markets → at home, a workforce already cut loose from the land by enclosure and stripped of legal protection by political economy.** The machine in Yorkshire is the part of that circuit John can actually reach with a hammer.

---

## 1. The world before — the commons & enclosure

Before the factory, most English country people lived partly off the **commons**. The open-field system scattered a family's land in narrow strips, and — crucially for the poor — a bundle of **customary common rights** let even the near-landless survive: grazing a cow or geese, gathering firewood (*estovers*), cutting peat (*turbary*), gleaning the fields after harvest. Around 1500, common and open-field land may have covered roughly **half** of England and Wales (Wikipedia, *Common land*).

**Enclosure** fenced those strips and commons into private holdings and extinguished the common rights. Two waves: a Tudor wave (15th–16th c.) driven by wool — Thomas More's *Utopia* (1516) gave it the image of sheep that "eat up… the very men themselves" — and the one that shaped the Luddite generation, **Parliamentary enclosure** (c.1750–1830), where landowners got an Act of Parliament to enclose a parish. **Scale: 1604–1914, over 5,200 enclosure acts enclosed ~6.8 million acres — about a fifth of England**, concentrated c.1760–80 and during the French wars c.1793–1815 (Wikipedia, *Inclosure Acts*). The General Acts of 1801 and 1845 industrialised the process itself.

The classic argument (the Hammonds, **E.P. Thompson**) is that enclosure was a decisive engine of **proletarianisation**: stripped of land and common rights, cottagers became wage-labourers — the workforce industry needed. **⚑ This is genuinely contested.** Revisionists (Chambers & Mingay; **Robert Allen**, *Enclosure and the Yeoman*; **Leigh Shaw-Taylor**) argue enclosure was often labour-intensive, its gains modest, and that a wage-labouring class was already forming beforehand. **J.M. Neeson** (*Commoners*, 1993) reasserts that losing the commons *was* a severe blow to the poor. **Marx** made English enclosure the centrepiece of "so-called primitive accumulation" in *Capital* I — the forcible "divorce of the producer from the means of production." (Marx is interpretation, not neutral chronicle — but it's foundational to how this era gets dramatised.)

**The bridge to John:** domestic-industry workers — Midlands framework-knitters, Yorkshire croppers, Lancashire weavers — were *not* pure proletarians. They typically combined craft earnings with a garden, a pig, a cow on the common. Enclosure ate that cushion, so when mechanisation and the wartime slump hit after 1811, there was no land to fall back on. (Hand-loom weavers collapsed from ~250,000 c.1800 to ~7,000 sixty years later.)

## 2. The global system — colonialism, slavery & cotton

The first factory industry ran on cotton, and the cotton was overwhelmingly **slave-grown**. British raw-cotton consumption exploded from ~2.5 million lb in 1750 to ~52 million lb in 1800 and 588 million lb by 1850 (Wikipedia, *Textile manufacture during the British Industrial Revolution*). Eli Whitney's **cotton gin (1793)** made short-staple slave cotton hugely profitable; by 1860 US plantations supplied nearly **90%** of the cotton spun in Lancashire — "King Cotton." In the Luddite years (1811–16), West Indian and rising US slave cotton already dominated.

The Atlantic trade enriched **Liverpool, Bristol, Glasgow**: in 1793–1807 Liverpool alone handled ~85% of British slave voyages. Whether slave/plantation profits *financed* industrialisation is the **⚑ Williams thesis** (Eric Williams, *Capitalism and Slavery*, 1944) — foundational, and genuinely **contested**. The strong causal/financing claim and Williams' "decline thesis" of abolition are disputed (Drescher's *Econocide*; Engerman's small profit-share estimates), while the broad *entanglement* of slavery and industrial growth is widely accepted (Inikori). Stage it as the live debate it is.

The other pole was **deindustrialisation**. Mughal India — Bengal, Dhaka muslin — had been a world textile leader (perhaps ~25% of world manufacturing output early in the 18th c.); under East India Company rule after Plassey (1757), asymmetric tariffs and machine-made Lancashire cloth turned India from the world's leading cloth *exporter* into a *buyer* of British manufactures and a supplier of raw cotton, opium, indigo. **⚑** Exact figures and how much was tariff vs. technology vs. agrarian shock are debated.

None of this was a free market: the **East India Company** was an armed corporation that conquered Bengal; the **Royal Navy** secured the shipping lanes; tariff law protected domestic spinners. Markets and raw materials were *made* by force and statute. **The immediate trigger of Luddite-era hunger was war economics:** Napoleon's Continental System and Britain's **Orders in Council (1807)** choked trade; with bad harvests (1810–11) and the looming War of 1812, the manufacturing districts fell into the worst slump in decades. The Orders were repealed 16–23 June 1812 — too late to stop the frame-breaking.

**The bridge to John:** the loom that replaces him is one node of a planetary circuit — the enslaved picker in Mississippi, the dispossessed weaver in Dhaka, the cropper in Yorkshire are all inside the same machine. John can reach only his local piece of it.

## 3. The new order — industrial capitalism & political economy

The factory **inverted** the old domestic "putting-out" system. Cottage artisans had worked "at their own pace, with their own tools, in their own cottages," paid by the piece. The factory concentrated labour under one roof where the **owner set the hours and the machine set the pace** — a transfer of control over time, pace and terms from worker to capital, and a **deskilling** of trades that had taken a seven-year apprenticeship to enter.

And the old **paternalist protections were dismantled exactly as the Luddites rose.** The Tudor **Statute of Artificers (1562)** had given magistrates power to fix wages and required seven-year apprenticeships; its **wage-fixing clauses were repealed in 1813**, its **apprenticeship clauses in 1814** (**⚑** full repeal not until 1825 — sources conflate these). When weavers asked instead for a statutory **minimum wage** (the 1808 cotton-weavers' bill), Parliament refused on explicit laissez-faire grounds — commerce must "take its own course."

Workers were simultaneously **forbidden to combine**: the **Combination Acts (1799/1800)** banned workers' associations to raise wages. Legal bargaining closed, organisation went underground into secret oath-bound cells — and **machine-breaking became bargaining by other means**. (Repealed only 1824.) Poor relief, via the **Speenhamland system (1795)**, topped up wages from the parish rates — which let employers pay below subsistence while ratepayers covered the gap (**⚑** its prevalence and wage-depressing effect are contested — Blaug).

The licence for non-intervention was **political economy as ideology**: **Adam Smith** (*Wealth of Nations*, 1776; **⚑** himself more nuanced than his laissez-faire reputation), **Malthus** (*Essay on Population*, 1798, used against poor relief), later **Ricardo** (subsistence "iron law" of wages). And there was **no political remedy**: pre-1832, voting required property; Manchester and Birmingham had *no* MPs while rotten boroughs returned two — so protest necessarily turned **extra-parliamentary**. The state answered with **spies and informers, the military, and the law** (next section).

## 4. The machinery question — technology as a class question

**This is the play's thesis in historical form, and the history backs it.** The Luddites were **not blind technophobes** — that caricature is exactly what social historians demolished. Eric Hobsbawm ("The Machine Breakers," *Past & Present*, 1952) showed the violence was **discriminating**: Lancashire wreckers spared small spinning-jennies and destroyed only the large ones "suitable only for use in factories" — they opposed machines "in the hands of the capitalist," not technology as such. His phrase for it: **"collective bargaining by riot"** (Hobsbawm, *verified* — not Thompson). The targeting maps onto specific grievances: Midlands knitters smashed the *wide frames* making shoddy "cut-ups" that undercut quality and apprenticeship; Yorkshire croppers smashed *shearing frames* and *gig mills* that deskilled their craft (the gig mill had been illegal by statute since Edward VI — they'd petitioned for a decade first); Lancashire weavers struck at *power looms*. Machines were attacked when used to **cut wages, debase quality, or evade customary/legal regulation** — not indiscriminately.

E.P. Thompson supplied the frame: the **"moral economy"** — a customary consensus of fair price and reciprocal obligation being "swept away by market forces." Luddism is the moral economy of the workplace.

Even the economists conceded the point. **David Ricardo added a chapter "On Machinery" to the 3rd edition of his *Principles* (1821)** publicly reversing himself: machinery substitution "is often very injurious to the interests of the class of labourers," and the workers' fear of it "is not founded on prejudice and error, but is conformable to the correct principles of political economy." (**⚑** he hedged in the same chapter — genuinely ambivalent; Hollander 2019.) Against him stood optimists like **Andrew Ure** (*The Philosophy of Manufactures*, 1835) and "compensation theory."

The distinction the play lives on: **technology-as-such vs. the social relations of its deployment — who owns it, who captures the gains, who bears the cost.** That is exactly the modern argument of **Acemoglu & Johnson** (*Power and Progress*, 2023): productivity gains don't automatically raise wages; distribution is a matter of power and choice — and they say plainly the Luddites "were right to worry." The popular bridge to AI runs through **Brian Merchant**, *Blood in the Machine* (2023) and **Gavin Mueller**, *Breaking Things at Work* (2021) — contemporary polemics, flag as interpretation. The agricultural sequel was **Captain Swing (1830)**, smashing threshing machines.

**⚑ The honest nuance — don't oversimplify into pure misery.** The **standard-of-living debate** is unresolved: optimists (Lindert & Williamson) estimate real wages roughly doubling 1780–1850; pessimists (Thompson, Hobsbawm) stress the gains came *late* (after the 1840s), with worse health, longer hours, high inequality. The dispute is really about **timing and distribution** — which *reinforces* the play's point: aggregate growth can coexist with ruin for the people the machine displaces *now*.

## 5. The Luddites themselves — events, people, repression

**The name was a myth weaponised.** "Ned Ludd" was apocryphal — a Leicester lad said (c.1779) to have smashed two stocking frames in a temper. There is no evidence he existed. When organised frame-breaking erupted in 1811, workers adopted **"General/King/Captain Ludd,"** an imaginary commander who "signed" proclamations from "Ned Lud's Office, Sherwood Forest" — deliberately invoking Robin Hood. The myth gave a leaderless, cellular movement one voice and shielded real organisers.

**Three regions, three trades, three grievances:**
- **Nottinghamshire/Midlands** — framework-knitters (stockingers); the grievance was the *abuse* of the stocking frame — wide frames worked by unapprenticed "colts" making cheap "cut-ups," plus wage-cutting and truck. First attack: **Arnold, 11 March 1811** (~63 frames of the most "obnoxious" hosiers; no violence). The most disciplined phase — fair employers' frames spared.
- **Yorkshire (West Riding)** — skilled **croppers/shearmen** finishing cloth with 50-lb shears, threatened with extinction by the **gig mill** and **shearing frame**. **11 April 1812:** ~150 men under cropper **George Mellor** attacked **William Cartwright's Rawfolds Mill** (Liversedge); repulsed by gunfire — **John Booth** and **Samuel Hartley** died. **28 April 1812:** Mellor's group assassinated mill-owner **William Horsfall** on Crosland Moor.
- **Lancashire** — cotton **handloom weavers** vs. **power looms**, fused with food riots; **Westhoughton Mill** burned, April 1812.

**Methods:** secret oaths, night drilling on the moors, disciplined raids, hundreds of letters signed "Ludd." Yorkshiremen carried **"Great Enoch,"** a sledgehammer named for blacksmith Enoch Taylor who made *both* the shearing frames and the hammers — **"Enoch made them, Enoch shall break them"** (a perfect motif: the maker is the breaker; see [[Themes & Motifs]]).

**Repression and the law:** the state committed an exceptionally large *domestic* military force (~12,000, largely militia/yeomanry) and leaned on paid **spies and informers**. **⚑ Do NOT say "more troops than Wellington had against Napoleon"** — a Hobsbawm-via-Darvall artefact of cherry-picking Wellington's *lowest* 1808 figure; by 1812 his Peninsular army far exceeded the home deployment (Anton Howes). Parliament rushed the **Frame Breaking Act (Destruction of Stocking Frames Act 1812**, royal assent **20 March 1812)**, making frame-breaking a **capital felony** — softened to transportation in 1814, death restored 1817. At the **York Special Commission (January 1813)**, **17 men were hanged**: Mellor, **William Thorpe**, **Thomas Smith** for Horsfall's murder (8 Jan); fourteen more for Rawfolds and arms raids (16 Jan); others transported. (**⚑** "60+ charged / ~30 acquitted" are approximate.)

**Byron:** in his maiden House of Lords speech (**27 February 1812**) opposing the Act, he defended men driven by "the capital crime of poverty" and asked "Is there not blood enough upon your penal code…?" He later wrote **"Song for the Luddites"** (1816): "down with all kings but King Ludd!" Both in [[Primary Sources — Luddite Era]].

**Afterlife:** "Luddite" only later hardened into a careless insult for anyone who fears technology — the opposite of what the croppers, who petitioned lawfully for a decade first, actually were.

## 7. Accuracy guardrails

> **House rule:** the play is **historically accurate** with only *minor* dramatisation. Myths play **no part** — not even as "period perception." Where a thing is genuinely debated by historians, we stage the *debate*, we don't pick a side and assert it.

### Myths — do NOT stage these as true (cut them)
- ❌ **"More troops against the Luddites than Wellington had against Napoleon."** False — a cherry-picked comparison to Wellington's *lowest* 1808 figure. Say instead: *an exceptionally large domestic deployment — roughly 12,000, mostly militia and yeomanry.*
- ❌ **A real "Ned Ludd" who smashed frames c.1779.** An invented figurehead; show the **name as a banner/collective fiction**, never a real man on stage.
- ❌ **"Independent landowners fell from 250,000 to 32,000."** Dubious, poorly-sourced — cut entirely.
- ❌ **"The Goose and the Common" as a 17th-century rhyme.** Earliest evidence is 1816 — use the rhyme, don't date it old.
- ⚠️ **Horsfall's "ride up to my stirrups in Luddite blood."** Attributed/traditional, not a verified quote — flag, don't assert.
- ⚠️ **"Oliver the Spy"** belongs to 1817 (Pentrich), **not** the 1811–13 Luddite peak — don't conflate.

### Genuine debates — stage as debate, don't assert one side
- **Williams thesis** (slavery *financed* industrialisation) — foundational but contested.
- **India deindustrialisation** — real, but exact figures and causes (tariff vs. tech vs. agrarian) are debated.
- **Enclosure → proletarianisation** — contested (Thompson/Neeson vs. Allen/Shaw-Taylor).
- **Speenhamland's prevalence/effect** — contested (Blaug).
- **Ricardo's reversal** — real and quotable, but he hedged in the same chapter (Hollander 2019).
- **Standard-of-living debate** — unresolved; really about *timing and distribution* (which actually *supports* the thesis: aggregate growth can coexist with ruin for the displaced).

### Get-the-detail-right (facts to keep straight)
- **Repeal dates:** 1813 (wage-fixing clauses), 1814 (apprenticeship clauses), full repeal 1825.
- **17 hanged at York**, Jan 1813 (well-attested); "60+ charged / ~30 acquitted" are approximate — phrase loosely.

## 8. How John carries all this (dramaturgy)
John doesn't lecture — he *lives* the system so the audience infers it:
- **The commons** he's losing (a pig, a garden, gleaning) shows up as the cushion that's already gone when the loom arrives — the enclosure strand, made domestic. (Connects the Loom Arrives / Cold Hearth scenes.)
- **The cotton** in his cloth can be named once — where it's picked, by whom — so the global circuit lands without a seminar.
- **The law** that protected his trade is repealed in his lifetime; his **lawful petition** fails (the Wage Cut scene) precisely because political economy has won — that's why he turns to the hammer.
- **"Enoch made them, Enoch shall break them"** is his thesis and the play's: the same hands that build the machine can break it; the fight is over *who it's for*. Mirror it in [[Liam]]'s world ([[Themes & Motifs]]).
- The **machinery question** (§4) is the load-bearing parallel to AI — keep it honest both centuries (see [[AI Coding & Labor]] and [[Braided Structure — Reference]]).
