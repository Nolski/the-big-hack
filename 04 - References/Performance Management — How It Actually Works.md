# Performance Management — How It Actually Works

> [!quote] Why it matters for *The Big Hack*
> [[Liam]] is not fired. He is **processed**. This note is the machinery of that processing — how a headcount number becomes a rating, a rating becomes a paper trail, and a paper trail becomes a name — so that [[12 - Below Expectations]], [[13 - The Performance Review]] and the offboarding (B8) are *procedurally accurate* and not a satirical guess. The play's argument only survives if a room full of Kiwicon engineers who have been through this recognise every step.

> [!warning] Verify before staging
> Specifics below are drawn from reporting and research through mid-2026 and are contested terrain, not settled fact. Company-specific claims are named with their source so they can be re-checked; treat the *mechanics* as reliable and the *numbers* as needing a fresh look before the show goes up. See also the jurisdiction flag at the bottom — the process described here is the American one, and the audience is not American.

## The one-sentence version

Performance management is not a judgement about a person's work. It is a **documentation process that makes an already-taken decision defensible**, and the thing being defended against is not the employee's disagreement — it is a lawsuit, a headline, and the next quarter's headcount review.

Everything else in this note follows from that. The review meeting is not where the outcome is decided; it is where the outcome is *delivered*. By the time Kristina opens the doc in [[13 - The Performance Review]], the decision is weeks old, the rating is locked, the wording has been through a People Partner, and the meeting has a script she is not allowed to leave. Her warmth is real. Her freedom is gone.

## The machinery, in order

### 1. The number arrives from above, with no names attached

Headcount pressure never arrives as a list of people. It arrives as a **shape**: flat headcount next year, a cost-per-engineer target, a margin the board wants to see, or — in the exact register the play already uses — a board member saying *"you probably need to performance manage a bunch of them out"* ([[11c - The Latest Model]]). Nobody at that level names anyone. They name a size and a mechanism, and the mechanism is a *choice*: you can do this as a redundancy, or you can do it as performance.

This is the most important thing to understand about the whole system: **choosing "performance" over "redundancy" is a decision made for reasons that have nothing to do with the performance of the people affected.**

### 2. Why "performance" and not "redundancy"

| Reason | What it actually buys the company |
|---|---|
| **Cost** | Performance exits pay little or no severance. A redundancy usually pays a formula (weeks per year of service) and often accelerates equity. |
| **Legal exposure** | A mass redundancy triggers notification duties (in the US, the WARN Act at scale) and invites disparate-impact analysis — someone runs the ages, genders and tenures of the cut list. Individual performance exits, spaced out, don't aggregate into that picture as cleanly. |
| **Optics, external** | "Layoffs" reads to the market as distress. "Raising the bar" reads as strength. The same cut, two press narratives. |
| **Optics, internal** | Survivors of a layoff update their résumés. Survivors of a performance cull update their behaviour. |
| **Deniability** | A redundancy is an admission the company over-hired. A performance exit says the company hired fine and the person failed. |

Reported practice: Meta's early-2025 cut of about 5% of staff (~3,600 people) was executed on performance-review grounds and internally framed as *"non-regrettable attrition"*; Amazon's use of PIPs ramped sharply — from under 2,000 people entering the first stage in April 2022 to a reported 3,300 a month by year end — immediately ahead of its large layoffs; Microsoft has attached a two-year no-rehire flag to performance exits, described internally as "good attrition." Formal written warnings across US workplaces are reported up roughly 30% since 2020. **Verify these before staging** — see Sources.

The euphemism to steal is **"non-regretted attrition"** (also "unregretted attrition," "URA"). It is a real term of art, it is spoken sincerely in real rooms, and it means: *people we are glad to lose.* It is the single most useful phrase in this note for the play, because it is funnier and colder than anything a writer would invent.

### 3. Calibration — the room that actually decides

Every large-enough company runs **calibration**: managers sit in a room (or a Zoom, or a spreadsheet) and rank their people against each other, so that ratings mean the same thing across teams. This is where Liam's fate is set, and he is not in the room. Neither, meaningfully, is his work.

> [!note] The play stages the spreadsheet variant, deliberately
> [[13 - The Performance Review]] opens on calibration as **a deadline, a shared sheet and a comment thread** — no meeting, no call, nobody in the same place, and in the staged version no voices either: a wordless minute of document before Kristina sits down with Liam. This is the common case at this company size and it dodges an argument the play cannot win: the moment a calibration *room* is staged, the audience starts checking who reports to whom and whether the people in it would really be there. Everything below still applies — the distribution, the zero-sum advocacy, the finite credibility, the drive-by opinion — it just happens asynchronously, in writing, where it is harder to argue with and much harder to remember happened.

What actually happens in calibration:

- Managers argue for their own people using **stories**, not data. The person with the best-told story wins, and the best-told story usually belongs to the most legible work.
- There is a **distribution** — soft or hard. Even where there's no literal quota, there is a strong expectation of what a healthy curve looks like, and a manager whose team is all "Exceeds" is told to re-look. Advocacy is therefore **zero-sum**: to protect one person you must expose another.
- A manager's credibility is a finite resource across the year. Spending it all on one difficult IC is a real cost, and everyone in the room knows it.
- Cross-team peers weigh in on people they have met three times. *"I've heard he's hard to work with"* is admissible and unanswerable.
- The output is not a decision to fire. It's a **rating** and a **rank order**. The firing decision gets made later, against that list, by someone who wasn't in the room.

Kristina's line — *"I can't be the only voice in the room"* ([[13 - The Performance Review]]) — is not a dodge. It is a literal description of calibration. She spent her credibility, lost, and is now delivering the room's verdict as if it were her own, because that is what managers are coached to do.

### 4. The dual axis — the trapdoor built for people like Liam

Nearly every modern review framework rates on **two independent axes**: *what* you delivered (impact, results, scope) and *how* you delivered it (collaboration, values, behaviours, "how we work"). The second axis exists for good reasons — it's what stops a high-output bully from being untouchable — and it is also the trapdoor.

Because the two axes are independent, **a company can concede that you are the best engineer it has and still exit you on the second axis, without contradiction.** That is exactly the structure of [[13 - The Performance Review]]: "your technical bar is the highest on this team" is not Kristina being nice before the knife. It is the first axis being scored honestly, so that the second axis can carry the whole weight. The visible line on the doc — "Collaboration: Below Expectations" — is doing the entire job.

The second axis is also, by construction, **subjective, socially sourced, and impossible to refute with evidence**. Liam can prove the race condition was real. He cannot prove that three people didn't feel friction. His entire defensive repertoire — being more correct, more thoroughly — is aimed at the axis that isn't the problem. That is the tragedy in one mechanical fact.

### 5. Manufacturing the record

The record is not found. It is **generated**, and mostly by asking questions that have predetermined answers.

- **Solicited peer feedback.** Someone asks a handful of colleagues a leading question — "was anything slowing your work down this half?", "anyone you found it hard to get unblocked by?" — and a word appears in three responses. Kristina's *"three people came to me, and the word they used was 'friction'"* is the honest version of this. Three people used the word because someone asked three people a question shaped like that word. Nobody lied. Nobody was even unkind.
- **Contemporaneous notes.** Managers are coached by HR to write things down as they happen — "document, document, document" — so that a case assembled at the end of a cycle looks like a case observed throughout it. The file often starts *after* the decision and is backfilled with real, true, selectively-chosen events.
- **The measurable proxy.** Anything with a number attached becomes the spine of the narrative, because numbers survive challenge. Review latency, PR cycle time, tickets closed, time-to-first-review, days-open on a blocking comment. *"A four-line fix sat for nine days"* is not an impression; it's a query someone ran. See §6 for why these numbers now point directly at senior reviewers.
- **The verbal-first ladder.** Feedback is deliberately given verbally first ("just some coaching"), then in writing, then formally. The employee experiences the first rung as a normal conversation. Legally and procedurally it was rung one of three. This is the single most-reported shock from people who've been through it: *"I had no idea that was the warning."*

### 6. The formal step, and the fork

Once a rating is low enough, the person goes onto a **PIP** (Performance Improvement Plan) or its softer-named cousins: a "focus plan," a "success plan," a 30/60/90. The stated purpose is rescue. The observed function, increasingly, is a documented runway to an exit — reporting now routinely describes the PIP as the quiet layoff mechanism rather than a rescue attempt.

Structurally, why they rarely work:

- Success criteria on the behaviour axis are unfalsifiable ("demonstrate improved collaboration"), so completion is a judgement call by the person who opened the plan.
- The person is now visibly marked. Colleagues route around them; the work that would prove the criteria stops arriving.
- Everyone treats the PIP as the start of a job search, including the manager. It is a *notice period that the employee pays for.*

And then there is **the fork**, which is where the process shows its hand most clearly. Very often the employee is offered a choice: go on the plan, or sign a **separation agreement** — some severance, in exchange for a **release of claims** and a **non-disparagement clause**. Framed as generosity, offered as kindness, and it does two things at once: it converts a contested dismissal into a purchased one, and it makes the person contractually quiet afterwards. It is also, materially, often the better deal for the employee, which is what makes it so effective.

The non-disparagement clause is worth flagging for [[19 - The Empty Apartment]]: part of the silence around Liam in B9 can be *his own*, bought and signed.

### 7. The exit conversation

By the time the meeting happens: the rating is locked, the wording is reviewed by a People Partner, the manager has been given talking points and told not to negotiate or apologise, IT has a scheduled task, and there is a calendar invite with a deliberately bland title. The manager's job in the room is to **deliver the message, absorb the reaction, and not reopen the decision.** Kristina's *"I hear you. Let's get time with your People Partner this week"* is precisely that manoeuvre, executed well and warmly. There is no version of that meeting Liam could have argued his way out of, because arguing is not one of the meeting's available functions.

## What's different for a senior engineer

Liam is ~38, staff-ish, twenty years in, **no direct reports by choice** ([[Liam]]). Every one of those facts changes the mechanics.

- **He is not judged on output.** Above senior, evaluation shifts to **scope, leverage, influence, multiplier effect** — how much *other people's* output you increase. This criterion is socially constructed, ambiguously measured, and therefore the easiest place in the whole framework to build a case. A senior engineer who solves the hardest problems personally is, in this frame, *doing junior work at a senior price* — and that sentence is said sincerely in calibration rooms.
- **His best work is illegible.** Prevented outages produce no artefact. The systems he quietly holds up generate tickets only when they break. The review reads what's in the tracker, and a career spent making sure nothing happens shows up as nothing happening.
- **"Bus factor" flips from asset to liability.** Being the only person who understands the critical system is described in his good years as indispensability and in his bad year as *"hasn't scaled himself,"* *"single point of failure,"* *"hoards context."* Same fact, opposite sign, and which sign it gets is decided by whether someone is currently building a case.
- **He has no political base.** He has no reports whose careers depend on him, no org to defend, no skip-level who owes him. In calibration, managers fight hardest for people whose loss would cost the manager something. An IC with no dependents is the cheapest possible loss.
- **He is expensive.** At a cost-per-unit-of-work framing, the top of the salary band is where the money is: one staff engineer is two or three mids. But "we cut him because he cost the most and had the most tenure" describes an age-discrimination claim, so it cannot be the stated reason. **The cost motive must be re-expressed as a performance narrative, and the second axis is where it goes.** This is not cynicism — it is the load-bearing reason the performance route exists at all.
- **More surface area.** Seniority means touching more teams, which means more peers sampled in a feedback round, which means more chances for a "collaboration" hit. The reward for breadth is exposure.

## What AI specifically changed (2025–26)

This is the layer the play is actually about, and it is genuinely new. Three things happened at once.

### AI adoption became a *rated criterion*, not a preference

Using the tools stopped being a matter of taste and became a line in the framework. Reported through 2025–26: **Meta** grading employees partly on AI-driven impact from the 2026 cycle, described as the first major firm to codify AI usage into review criteria; **JPMorgan** tying performance outcomes to AI-tool adoption across roughly 65,000 engineers and technologists, citing its own internal measurement of 10–20% productivity gains as the justification for mandating the tools; **Shopify**'s 2025 memo requiring teams to demonstrate that AI *cannot* do a job before requesting headcount for it. Microsoft, Google and Amazon pushed adoption hard without (reportedly) codifying it as explicitly.

The consequence for Liam is exact and should be stated plainly in the writers' room: **skepticism about a tool is now a documentable performance issue.** Not an opinion, not friction — a criterion, with a box, and a rating. In [[11b - Best Practices]] Kristina's *"I just need you on board, Liam"* is warm and personal. Under this regime it is also, quietly, the name of a rating line.

### Adoption became *telemetry*, and telemetry measures the wrong thing

Companies now have dashboards: seat licences and whether they're active, suggestion-acceptance rates, share of PRs with AI-assisted commits, agent-authored line counts, time-to-first-review, DORA metrics sliced by AI usage. The measurement is **asymmetric** — it is far easier to measure *whether you used the tool* than *whether the work was good*. So an engineer who uses the tool badly and ships fast scores above one who doesn't use it and ships well.

Liam's rig from [[11b - Best Practices]] — a quantized local model, no tools, no network, stubs only — is the worst possible position on this dashboard. It is genuinely the most compliant setup on the team and it registers as **near-zero adoption**, because the telemetry runs through the corporate seats he isn't using. He is off the chart in the literal sense.

### The bottleneck moved to the reviewer — and reviewers have names

The productivity picture is contested, and the contestedness is fuel for the play rather than a problem for it:

- **METR's randomized controlled trial** (July 2025): 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 tasks in repositories they'd worked in for ~5 years, using Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet. They were **19% slower** with AI tools — and estimated afterwards that they had been **20% faster**. METR itself now labels the result historical, which the play should honour: it was true of those models, and [[11c - The Latest Model]] is built on exactly that moving floor.
- **Gartner**: roughly one in five AI investments delivers measurable ROI.
- Industry benchmark reporting for 2026 describes high-adoption teams completing ~21% more tasks and merging ~98% more PRs — while **PR review time rose ~91%**, relocating the constraint to human approval. The same reporting claims senior engineers realise several times the gains juniors do.

That third bullet is the mechanical heart of Liam's review. When generation gets cheap and review doesn't, **throughput rises until it hits the reviewer, and the reviewer becomes the visible constraint on somebody's chart.** The most careful person on the team becomes, measurably and truthfully, the thing slowing the team down. *"Nine days on a four-line fix"* is not a smear — it is the correct output of a correctly-built dashboard pointed at a man doing his job properly.

And note the mirror to [[16 - The Wage Cut]]: John's hand-woven cloth is genuinely better and the *rate* for it collapses anyway. Liam's review is genuinely correct and the *price* of correctness collapses anyway. Same beat, same century apart.

### The cuts don't need the productivity to be real

Reported 2026 numbers: tech layoffs running around 1,100+ a day, roughly double the prior year's pace; ~142,000 by late May, ~170,000 by mid-year; Meta, Google, Amazon, Oracle, Block, Atlassian, Pinterest and Salesforce among those explicitly linking cuts to AI productivity. Simultaneously, profitable companies were cutting to fund an enormous AI capex build-out, and the same reporting notes the cuts have not demonstrably improved returns.

For the play, the honest formulation — and it should be said somewhere in the room, in someone's mouth — is: **the layoff does not require the productivity gain to be real. It requires the productivity story to be sayable to the board.** The gap between hype and measured output is not a flaw in the mechanism; it's the mechanism's operating margin.

## How to use it — craft notes

- **Nobody ever says the true sentence.** No one will say "we are exiting you because you didn't adopt the tool and you cost the most." Every step launders the reason into the next vocabulary. The play's job is to let the audience watch the laundering, not to have a character announce it. (Per [[Writing Didactic Drama — Craft Notes]] and the show's no-narration rule.)
- **The chain is not one chain, and that is the ending.** Everything above describes a performance process that is legible, appealable in theory, and aimed at a person. It is not what removes him. A second process — a scored reduction, run off tracked fields for consistency and legal defensibility, precisely so that no individual is making a call about an individual — runs beside it, never reads its output, and gets there first. **Both are working as designed.** The performance chain's whole pathos depends on it being the thing that matters; the reduction is what actually decides, and it is not evaluating him at all, it is sorting a column. When writing anything downstream of this, the rule is: **do not let the two systems acknowledge each other**, and do not give the reduction an author. Dana's honest answer — "I don't know that it's a who" — is the accurate one.
- **What gets tracked decides who survives, and it is never what the work is.** The reduction's columns exist because someone could build them: commits, merges, tickets, adoption percentages, last cycle's rating. Nothing a reviewer prevents leaves a trace, because the whole point of a good review is that the incident does not happen and there is no row for an incident that didn't. The perverse consequence is exact and should be staged, not stated: **being told to review more lowers your authored-changes figure**, so complying with the performance process degrades your standing in the reduction process. Nobody built that. It is what happens when two instruments measure the same person for different reasons.
- **Each step is defensible alone; the horror is the sum.** This matches the "keep honest" note in [[Liam Arc]] exactly, and the machinery above is *why* it's true rather than just a stylistic preference. The board member is describing a market. Kristina really did advocate. The peers really did feel friction. The metric really does say nine days. HR really is following a policy that exists to protect people. Nobody in the chain does anything a reasonable person wouldn't.
- **Give the second axis its due.** The behaviour axis exists because high-output bullies are real. Liam *did* sit on a four-line fix for nine days. If the play treats the collaboration criterion as pure pretext, it loses the steelman and becomes the thing it's satirising.
- **The feedback round is written.** [[12 - Below Expectations]] stages it: somebody asks a leading question and Liam's colleagues answer it honestly, which is how the silence becomes a document. Brendan answering a survey truthfully is a worse betrayal than Brendan saying nothing, and it's the same man from [[11c - The Latest Model]] who already knows performance-management talk is in the air. The scene adds one thing this note didn't anticipate and should: **the tool writes the summary.** Several careful, contradictory answers become one generated sentence — "multiple peers cite friction and slow review turnaround" — that no human typed and no human can be asked about. Where the doc says the record is *manufactured*, the current reality is that a model does the manufacturing, and the anonymity notice is what makes it unauditable.
- **The record can't be appealed, even by someone doing everything right.** The scene's third movement is the part this note was missing, and it belongs in §5. Liam reads the generated sentence, works out correctly that "multiple" plus a four-person team is not anonymity, and then does the only sane thing available: he rings a colleague and asks what to fix. **There is no step in the process designed to receive that call.** The peer round is closed, the summary has no author, the manager isn't in the conversation, and the colleague he happens to ring is one of the sources. He is given a true, kind, useless piece of advice — a paraphrase of the thing that ended him — writes it down, and thanks the person for it. The point for the rest of the chain: *the process does not fail because the subject is defensive or unaware.* It fails identically when the subject is diligent, unsuspicious and grateful, which is far worse and much harder to dismiss.
- **And the record is where the advocacy dies, too.** The prologue to [[13 - The Performance Review]] adds the other half: Kristina's defence of Liam is not spoken and lost in a room, it is **typed into a comment, agreed with, and marked Resolved**. Two things about that are worth keeping in mind when writing the rest of the chain. First, the one argument that would actually have explained his adoption number — that his local rig is the only setup on the team complying with the security policy he wrote — she types out and deletes, because in writing, under her name, it reads as an excuse. Second, agreement costs nothing and changes nothing: "I don't disagree with any of that" is the most common sentence in the entire machine, and it is always true.
- **Keep HR unvillainous.** The People Partner is not cruel; they are a person executing a procedure designed by lawyers, and they are frequently the only person in the chain who tells Liam a straight fact. The register is "scripted, gentle, immovable" ([[Modern World — Supporting]]) — hold it.
- **The numbers are the comedy.** Nine days. Four lines. Three people. 21% more PRs, 91% longer reviews, 19% slower, 20% faster. Precision is funnier and colder than adjectives, and this audience will check.

Under NZ employment law a performance dismissal requires a genuine, fair process — notice of concerns, a real opportunity to improve, the right to representation — and an unjustified one is a personal grievance with real remedies. Read as NZ, this machinery looks either foreign or unlawful, which is a *different* and weaker point than the one the play is making.
> **The call:** the company is explicitly **US-domiciled and Liam is on a US contract**, consistent with the CEO/board register already established. The vocabulary is therefore unrestricted — PIP, at-will, 30/60/90, release of claims, non-regretted attrition, do-not-rehire — and a Kiwicon crowd recognises all of it from reporting and watches from one step outside. Don't hedge it back toward neutrality in later scenes; the specificity is the point.
