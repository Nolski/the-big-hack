# Writing Didactic Drama — Craft Notes

> [!quote] Why it matters for *The Big Hack*
> Our whole problem in one line: teach the political economy of the Luddites ↔ modern software labour through real character arcs and natural dialogue, **without it turning into a lecture**. This note collects the acclaimed examples and named techniques that solve exactly that, and maps each to our show. Companion to [[Concept & Thesis]], [[Themes & Motifs]], and [[Inspiration — Tone & Form]].

## The one principle everything serves
**The political economy belongs to the *audience*, not the characters.** [[Liam]] and [[John]] each understand only their own small patch — the bug, the loom, the rent. The braid (see [[Braided Structure — Reference]]) and the narrator supply the system they can't see. That gap is **dramatic irony**, and it's the engine that lets the show be deeply educational while no character ever lectures. The instant a character accurately diagnoses the system they're trapped in, it's cringe; the instant they *misdiagnose* it ("the law will sort it out"; "being right is safe"), it's tragedy — and the audience learns more.

## The anti-cringe rules (from the masters)
- **Steelman the antagonist — give the machine its best argument.** Shaw's *Major Barbara* ("A Discussion in Three Acts") makes the arms-dealer Undershaft the most persuasive person on stage. Audiences forgive a thesis when they can't tell whose side the author is on. Our [[Historical World — Supporting|Cartwright]] (= "progress") and [[Modern World — Supporting|Kristina]] (= real conversion data) must be *right on their own terms*. Never stupid, never cackling.
- **Put the ideology *inside* the character.** Willy Loman's tragedy is that he has internalised the system that discards him ("a man is not a piece of fruit"). That's how political economy becomes *character*, not message: the worker who believes in the thing killing him. We already have it — John's faith in fair dealing; Liam's belief that excellence is a shield.
- **Subtext over statement.** Bury the point under what people actually say; leave half the conversation to silence. Kill any line that could be a tweet about capitalism. No "As you know, Bob."
- **Comedy is the Trojan horse.** *Enron*, *The Big Short*, *Serious Money* all smuggle dense economics in on satire and spectacle; the laugh lowers the guard, then it curdles. This is our spine — lean harder (see the curdle ledger in [[Themes & Motifs]]).

## The acclaimed playbook — techniques to steal
- **Enron (Lucy Prebble, 2009; Best New Play).** Dramatises *mark-to-market accounting* by **giving abstractions bodies** — the toxic-debt vehicles ("the Raptors") are literal predators on stage; the trading floor is a chorus. → We deliberately keep the AI tool and the steam looms *bodiless* — recurring forces felt through deployment and ambient presence, never figures on stage; consider instead giving a modern abstraction a body (the velocity metric / burn-down chart as a thing that eats people).
- **The Big Short (McKay, 2015).** When a concept is unavoidable, it **breaks the fourth wall and owns the artifice** — Margot Robbie in a bath explains mortgage-backed securities; Gosling stares down the lens: *this matters, listen.* → Our narrator/Agent V.O. can run a 20-second self-aware "here's the one thing about the Frame-Breaking Act"; owning that it's an explainer makes it land instead of clunk.
- **Brecht — epic theatre.** The **Verfremdungseffekt** (distancing) deliberately breaks immersion so the audience *thinks* about social mechanics; **gestus** is the one physical gesture that shows a character's *economic position* (the servant's bow, the boss's posture). The **Lehrstück** (learning-play) was performed then debated. → The braid and direct address ARE distancing devices — use them to think, not just feel. Find our gestures of class: Liam's camera-off black square, John's loom rhythm.
- **The Lehman Trilogy (Massini/Power; 2022 Best Play).** Three actors, **direct-address third-person narration**, fluid role/age switching, turn 160 years of capitalism into intimate storytelling. → Narration is allowed and can be gorgeous; one device (our V.O.) carrying the centuries is a feature, not a cheat.
- **Caryl Churchill.** *Light Shining in Buckinghamshire* drops the **verbatim Putney Debates** (real 1647 transcript) straight into the play; *Serious Money* satirises finance in **rhyming verse**; *Cloud Nine* is already our structural cousin. → Use our primary sources verbatim as set pieces (Byron's speech, the Weavers' songs, Luddite proclamations — [[Primary Sources — Luddite Era]], [[The Weavers — Pulled Passages]]).
- **Documentary / verbatim theatre (The Laramie Project; Anna Deavere Smith).** Built from real testimony; *reality* makes the argument, and it can't be dismissed as caricature. → Salt the modern story with real artifacts: an actual layoff email, a Slack thread, an earnings-call transcript.
- **Death of a Salesman (Miller).** The canonical worker-made-obsolete, taught purely through one family's love and self-deception — no politics spoken aloud. → Our North Star for *how much to leave unsaid.*

## The sharpest single move for us — documentary juxtaposition
Formalise the cross-century echo already in [[Themes & Motifs]] into a recurring device: cut a **real** 1810s political-economy line directly against its **real** 2025 twin, and let the audience do the analysis —
- Andrew Ure, *The Philosophy of Manufactures* (1835), on the factory as a "vast automaton" ↔ a real "agentic workforce / AI sets us free" launch post.
- The magistrate's "the law is the law" ↔ HR's "this isn't a reflection of your value."
- Ricardo conceding machinery "is often very injurious to … the labourers" (1821) ↔ a modern exec admitting the quiet part.

Used 4–5 times across the hour, this is the educational backbone — and it's impossible to find cringey because every word is real. (Accuracy rules still apply — see [[Luddites — Period & Conditions]] §7.)

## How to use it
- Drafting any "concept" beat: first ask *can a character simply not understand this instead of explaining it?* (dramatic irony). If it must be stated, hand it to the narrator/Agent and **own the artifice** (Big Short).
- Drafting [[Historical World — Supporting|Cartwright]] / [[Modern World — Supporting|Kristina]]: write their best, sincerest case (Shaw). If the audience nods along before they wince, it's working.
- Building set pieces: reach for **verbatim** primary text (Churchill / verbatim theatre) and **embodied abstraction** (Enron) before reaching for explanation.
